# Writing guide for Paper 2, v3

*Sentence frames, reporting templates, and word choices for writing the paper in
plain English. Written for the first author; useful for anyone editing the
draft.*

---

## 1. Five rules for every sentence

1. **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has "and" joining two ideas, split it.
2. **About twenty words.** Longer sentences are where errors hide.
3. **A verb in every sentence.** "Phase explains 76% of the variance." Not
   "Phase: 76% of variance."
4. **Define a term before you use it.** "Hedges' g, the difference between two
   means in standard-deviation units, was 3.44."
5. **Numbers belong in tables.** In prose, give one or two numbers per sentence
   and point to the table for the rest.

---

## 2. The skeleton of every results section

Use the same five headings every time. Readers learn the pattern and read faster.

- **Question.** One sentence, ending in a question mark.
- **What we compared** (or *What we correlated*, *What we fitted*). The
  variables, the groups, the table and figure numbers.
- **Result.** The numbers, in the order of the table.
- **What it means.** One or two sentences, in plain words.
- **What it does not mean.** The claim a careless reader might make, and why it
  is not supported.

---

## 3. Reporting templates

Fill the blanks. Keep the order.

**Means (descriptive).**
> Mean daily deaths were [123.6] (SD [47.0]) during [Major Combat] and [13.7]
> (SD [21.9]) during [the First Ceasefire].

**One-way ANOVA.**
> A one-way ANOVA showed that mean daily deaths differed across the four phases,
> F([3], [166]) = [172.3], p [< .001], η² = [0.76].

**Two-group comparison (Welch t-test).**
> [Major Combat] averaged [109.9] more deaths per day than [the First Ceasefire]
> (95% CI [94.3] to [125.6]), Welch t([46.7]) = [14.11], Holm-adjusted p [< .001],
> Hedges' g = [3.44].

**Correlation.**
> In [Days 40–170], strike locations per day and Iranian civilian deaths per day
> were positively correlated, r = [+0.52], p [< .001], n = [131] days.

**Simple regression slope.**
> Within [Days 40–170], each additional strike location was associated with
> [0.29] additional Iranian civilian deaths that day (95% CI [0.21] to [0.37]).

**Regression with an interaction.**
> The strikes × damage interaction was positive, b = [0.0145] (HAC SE [0.0053]),
> HAC p = [.006]. The strike slope was [−0.46] (p = [.176]) at [31] damaged
> facilities and [+0.83] (p = [.011]) at [307].

**A check that does not reach significance.**
> With the [HSSI] as the damage measure, the interaction had the same sign but
> did not reach significance, b = [0.0059], HAC p = [.32].

**Projection.**
> Under published ratios of [3:1] to [15:1], the documented toll of
> [3,166–3,636] implies a total of [12,664] to [58,176]. At the often-cited
> [4:1] ratio, the total would be [15,830–18,180].

## 4. Words to use and words to avoid

| Use | Avoid | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "was associated with" | "caused", "led to" | The data show associations between daily aggregates, not causes. |
| "consistent with resilience erosion" | "proves resilience erosion", "shows the system collapsed" | The mechanism is not tested directly. |
| "one of the two damage measures reached significance" | "both measures confirm" | The HSSI interaction is not significant (p = .32). |
| "projection" | "estimate", "our figure" | The ratio is imported from other wars. |
| "a floor" | "the death toll" | The counted number is the smaller part of the toll. |
| "range … reference point" | "the number is 17,000" | Present the range; use 4:1 only as a reference. |
| "large effect (g = 3.44)" | "highly significant" | Significance says the difference is unlikely to be chance; the effect size says how big it is. Report both, in that order. |
| "not significant (p = .176)" | "no effect", "proves there is no relationship" | A non-significant result means the data do not show a relationship; it does not prove the absence of one. |
| "strike locations per day" | "strikes" (alone, in a results sentence) | The exposure is a count of distinct places struck. |
| "documented deaths" | "deaths" | Every death count here is a reported figure. |
| "the daily series" and "the dashboard counter" | "the data" | There are two accountings; name the one you mean. |

## 5. Grammar and style points for statistical English

- **Tense.** Past tense for what we did and found: "We compared", "The slope
  was". Present tense for what a table shows and for general truths: "Table 4
  shows", "Wars kill in two ways".
- **"Data" is plural** in formal scientific English: "the data show", not "the
  data shows". "Dataset" is singular.
- **Fewer / less.** "Fewer deaths" (countable). "Less damage" (uncountable).
- **Between / among.** "Between two phases"; "among the four phases".
- **Compared with.** "Compared with the ceasefire, Major Combat had …". Not
  "compared to".
- **Affect / effect.** "Damage affects the slope" (verb). "The effect of damage"
  (noun). "Effect size" (noun phrase).
- **Hyphens.** "day-to-day variance", "health-system damage", "strike-day count"
  when the phrase comes before a noun; no hyphen after: "the health system was
  damaged".
- **Numbers.** Use numerals for all statistics and counts: "3 phases", "40 days".
  Spell out a number that starts a sentence, or restructure so it does not.
- **p-values.** No leading zero and three decimals: p = .006; p < .001. Never
  p = .000.
- **Effect sizes and correlations.** Two decimals: g = 3.44, r = −0.41.
- **Percentages.** One decimal in the text: 79.6%. Whole numbers in speech.
- **Ranges.** An en dash with no spaces: 3,166–3,636; Days 1–40.
- **Articles.** "The daily series" (a specific thing). "A strike location" (any
  one). "Facility damage" (uncountable, no article). "The health system" (one
  specific system).
- **Which / that.** "The measure that reached significance" (defines which one).
  "The HSSI, which counts 21 events, …" (adds information; commas).
- **Avoid "very", "really", "extremely".** Give the number instead.
- **Avoid "it is important to note that".** Just note it.

## 6. Frames for the sections you own

**Section 1.2, the ward-side paragraph.**
> When a mass-casualty event reaches a ward, the staff work with what is in the
> building at that moment: [what]. There is no time to [what cannot be done].
> The [resource] that is used up on the first night is not there on the second.
> In this war the health system met its largest wave of casualties in the first
> [number] weeks, and it carried the cost of that wave into [what followed].

**Section 4.4, the water passage.**
> On Day 140 the intake of the Bonji desalination plant was destroyed. About
> 10,000 people in 20 villages lost their drinking water. Households [what they
> do next]. Within two to three weeks, [who] arrive at [where] with [what
> illness]. The facility they reach may be short of [what]. Some of them die.
> None of those deaths appears in a strike count. The same is true of [the
> dialysis patient …] and [the delivery …].

**Section 5.2, sustaining care during the war.**
> Protecting [theatres / blood supply / power to hospitals / staff] during the
> war has direct life-saving value because [reason drawn from Finding 2]. In
> practice this means [two or three concrete measures].

## 7. Checklist before sending a draft

- [ ] Every number in the text matches a table (run `bash run_all.sh`; the last
      step checks the tables).
- [ ] Every statistical term is defined on first use or is in the glossary.
- [ ] Every results section has the five headings in order.
- [ ] No sentence claims a cause. Search the draft for "caused", "led to",
      "because of the damage".
- [ ] The word "estimate" does not appear near the projection. Search for
      "estimate".
- [ ] The HSSI result is described as "same direction, not significant".
- [ ] The 4:1 ratio is called a reference point, and the range appears before it.
- [ ] p-values have no leading zero. Search for "p = 0."
- [ ] Sentences longer than about 25 words have been split.
- [ ] Drafting notes have been deleted (Word: Find, style "Drafting Note").
