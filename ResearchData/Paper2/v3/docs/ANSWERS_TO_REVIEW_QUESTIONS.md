## 2. Finding 2: "how do I read the HSSI result honestly?"

**Your proposed sentence is correct.** You can write, as you suggested, that
*the evidence is consistent with resilience erosion, with one of the two measures
reaching significance.*

The fuller version, which Section 5.3 of the manuscript uses, names the measures:

> "As facility damage accumulated, each additional strike was associated with
> more civilian deaths. The evidence is consistent with a health system losing
> its ability to absorb shocks. Of the two damage measures, the facility-damage
> curve gives a significant interaction; the coarser 21-event index points the
> same way but does not reach significance."

Three details to keep straight when you write in your own voice:

1. Quote the HAC (autocorrelation-robust) p-value for the HSSI interaction,
   p = .32. The ordinary p-value is .078, which is borderline, but the paper
   quotes HAC p-values throughout and should not switch for this one result.
2. What the HSSI does not establish is the *rate* of steepening (the
   interaction). Its end point is similar: the strike slope at the top of the
   HSSI is +0.80 (p = .023), against +0.83 in the primary model.
3. Do not say "both measures confirm the effect" and do not say "the HSSI
   contradicts the effect". It points the same way and is not significant.
   That is the whole statement.

Two sentences to avoid: "facility damage caused strikes to become deadlier"
(claims a mechanism the data cannot prove) and "the system collapsed" (a word
the data do not measure).

One change from version 2 that affects how you describe Finding 2: version 2
gave the slopes as 0.18 → 0.63 → 1.07 at "mean ± 1 SD" of damage. The +1 SD
level (116%) is above the highest damage that occurred, so version 3 reports the
slope at levels that did occur: −0.46 at 31 damaged facilities (not significant)
to +0.83 at 307 (p = .011). Use the version 3 numbers.

## 3. Finding 3: "should I anchor on 4:1 or present the range?"

**Present the range. Use 4:1 only as a reference point.** The reasons:

- The ratio is imported from studies of other wars. It was not measured in this
  one. A single number invites a reader to quote it as our estimate.
- The range is the honest statement of what the literature supports: 3:1 to
  15:1, which for Iran gives 12,664 to 58,176 total deaths.
- 4:1 is useful as a reference because it is the figure most often cited, and
  because a reader needs one row to hold on to. Call it "the often-cited 4:1
  ratio" or "the reference scenario". Never "our estimate".

Table 10 and Figure 5 already do this: four ratios, both direct-toll bounds, the
4:1 row labelled as the reference. Your text should follow the same order:
floor, then range, then reference.

A sentence you can use:

> "Under the ratios reported in past conflicts, 3:1 to 15:1, the documented
> Iranian toll of 3,166–3,636 implies 12,664 to 58,176 total deaths. At the
> often-cited 4:1 ratio the figure would be 15,830–18,180. The count is the
> floor; the ratio decides how far above it the total lies."

## 4. "Why do the two death counts differ (14.8% for Iran, 43.9% for Lebanon)?"

**The short answer:** the two series are built differently, and the gap is a
different kind of gap in each country. Box 1 (Section 2.5) and Table 9 in the
manuscript set this out; Figure 4 draws it.

*How the two series are built.* The daily series assigns deaths to the day they
were reported and is never revised. The dashboard counter is a running total
that was updated whenever a new cumulative figure was published, and it could be
moved to a different source's figure.

*Iran: a source difference, settled on Day 57.* By Day 40 the daily series had
3,125 Iranian deaths; the counter had 9,100, because it was tracking the high
totals published by Hengaw. On Day 57 the counter was re-anchored to the Legal
Medicine Organization's forensic count (9,226 → 3,375) and later moved to a
verified 3,636. After Day 40 the daily series added only 41 deaths. So the
470-death gap at Day 170 is the difference between two sources' totals for the
same early period. It is not a lag in new deaths, and it is not the dataset
aggregating differently.

*Lebanon: deaths confirmed later and added to a running total.* At Day 40 the
two accountings agreed (1,781 vs 1,751). During the First Ceasefire the daily
series added 1,200 Lebanese deaths, while the ministry's running total, which the
counter follows, rose by 2,550. Almost the whole gap opened in those 89 days. A
ministry total includes deaths confirmed after the day of the event: bodies
recovered, patients who die in hospital days later, and periodic revisions. The
daily series does not go back to add them. "Reporting lag" is a fair short
description; "deaths confirmed after the day of the event and added to a running
total" is the fuller one.

*So which of your three options is it?* For Iran, source divergence. For
Lebanon, mainly reporting lag in the sense above. In both countries the gap is
structural, because it comes from how the two series are built, but the
structural difference produces a different kind of gap in each case.

*What to say if a reader asks:* "The dataset keeps two accountings of direct
deaths. They are built differently and we carry both as bounds. For Iran the
gap comes from a change of source that was settled on Day 57. For Lebanon it
comes from deaths confirmed after the day of the event and added to the
ministry's running total during the ceasefire. We can see when each gap opened;
we cannot see inside the sources' counting."
