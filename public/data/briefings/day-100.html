Situation Summary

Day 100 finds the war in a stable disequilibrium: a blockade-plus-skirmish standoff centered on the Strait of Hormuz, with the high-intensity strike campaign of late February through early May now firmly behind it. June 5–6 distilled the new pattern into a single repeating cycle — IRGC one-way attack drones harassed Hormuz shipping; CENTCOM downed six of them over the two days and struck Iranian coastal surveillance radar at Qeshm Island and Goruk; and Iran answered with seven ballistic missiles plus drones at U.S. bases in Kuwait and Bahrain. Every projectile was intercepted, and the entire exchange produced zero casualties on either side. Iran condemned the U.S. radar strikes as a “clear violation” of the fragile ceasefire; Washington framed them as self-defense of maritime traffic. By June 6, President Trump was describing the war as “largely finished … one way or the other.”

The decisive front, however, is economic rather than kinetic. United Against Nuclear Iran reported that Iran’s May oil exports collapsed about 93 percent to just 2.01 million barrels for the entire month — an average of roughly 65,000 barrels a day — valued at $219 million, with no crude successfully running the U.S. blockade. The interdiction net widened east of Hormuz: forces staging from the expeditionary sea base USS John L. Canley boarded the sanctioned tanker MT Davina near Sri Lanka, the sixth ship blocked since the blockade began. This economic vise — not airstrikes — is now Washington’s primary instrument of coercion, and its durability depends on U.S. political will rather than on munitions stockpiles. Diplomacy, by contrast, is frozen on sequencing: Mohsen Rezaei, a military adviser to Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, told CNN that any deal hinges on the United States first releasing $24 billion in frozen Iranian assets, that talks are “at a deadlock,” and that “the ball is in Trump’s court,” with Tehran having suspended negotiations pending a full Lebanon ceasefire.

Markets, meanwhile, have decoupled from the war. Wall Street’s worst day since October arrived on June 5 — the S&P 500 fell 2.64 percent to 7,383.74 and the Nasdaq sank 4.18 percent — but it was a chip-led technology rout (Broadcom, Nvidia, Micron) compounded by a hot May jobs report of 172,000 that lifted rate-hike odds, not a war shock; the Hormuz exchanges barely registered, and the index remains up about 7.9 percent on the year. Oil drifted lower in sympathy, Brent settling at $93.09 and WTI at $90.54, and AAA national gasoline eased to $4.174. The one fresh casualty event came on the Lebanon front, where an Israeli strike killed three Lebanese Army soldiers, including a brigadier general, near Kfar Tebnit — the IDF said the vehicle had moved “suspiciously” and denied intentional targeting. As the war crosses its hundredth day, the base case has shifted from resolution toward a frozen conflict; the principal danger is no longer a planned escalation but a “lucky” Iranian hit on a tanker or a base that breaks the zero-casualty pattern and re-couples the war to the markets.

Ceasefire Whiplash: A New Lebanon Truce Meets Continued Fire

The most consequential development of June 4 was a ceasefire that began unraveling at the moment it was announced. The United States said Israel and Lebanon had agreed to implement a truce — an end to Hezbollah fire, a Hezbollah withdrawal south of the Litani River, and security zones placed under exclusive Lebanese control, with both sides to resume negotiations later in the month. Within hours the agreement was being tested: Israeli strikes continued across southern Lebanon, and Hezbollah, which had not signed on, rejected the latest terms outright. The pattern was familiar — a diplomatic announcement front-running the facts on the ground — but the stakes were higher, because Lebanon has become the hinge on which the wider U.S.–Iran track now turns.

On the U.S.–Iran axis, the kinetic tempo actually eased even as the diplomacy stalled. CENTCOM reported intercepting “an additional wave of Iranian drones” aimed at American forces in Kuwait, with no Americans or assets harmed; Iran’s navy claimed it had struck a U.S. CENTCOM facility “responsible for recent U.S. actions,” a claim CENTCOM denied. Notably, the United States conducted no new strike on Iranian territory — a clear de-escalation from the June 2–3 window, when CENTCOM hit a ground-control station on Qeshm Island and fired a Hellfire at an Iranian oil tanker near the strait. The tentative sixty-day extension framework reached May 24–28, mediated through Pakistan with a separate nuclear track, remained unsigned, its disputed clauses on enriched-uranium disposal, Hormuz language, and frozen-fund access still open.

The juxtaposition captured the war’s defining instability: a de-escalatory gesture on one front — a Lebanon ceasefire, and salvos against U.S. forces intercepted rather than landing — layered over an unresolved core, an unsigned U.S.–Iran MoU and a Lebanon track Israel refuses to fold into it. Iran’s chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, made the linkage explicit, warning that Tehran could abandon the talks for “confrontation” if Israeli strikes on Lebanon continued — precisely the strikes that resumed within hours of the announcement. The cleanest near-term test remains whether the Lebanon truce survives contact and whether President Trump’s repeated signal that the MoU could be signed “over the weekend” produces a signature or yet another empty deadline.

Kuwait Again: An Intercepted Drone Wave and Dueling Claims

President Trump’s framing ran in the opposite direction. He had said a day earlier that a deal “could happen over the weekend” and that Iran had “already agreed they’re not going to have a nuclear weapon” — optimism that the negotiators’ own readouts did not support. The tentative sixty-day extension framework reached in late May remained unsigned, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s House testimony on department priorities underscored how much of the war’s machinery — sanctions enforcement, frozen-asset disposition, the unwinding of the economic blockade — still runs through unresolved questions. The domestic backdrop continues to tighten. A day earlier the House had passed an Iran war-powers resolution 215–208 — the first such measure to clear a chamber, with four Republicans crossing party lines — and attention now turns to whether the Senate takes up its own, more procedurally potent version. The war reaches its hundredth day on June 7, and the administration is managing a contested ceasefire that it describes as having effectively concluded even as CENTCOM, Iran, and independent reporting document continuing strikes. That framing gap — peace by one account, a frequently-violated truce by every other — is the war’s most basic ambiguity, and June 4 did nothing to resolve it: a Lebanon ceasefire announced and immediately tested, a drone wave intercepted but launched, and a negotiation that is simultaneously “open” and going nowhere.
