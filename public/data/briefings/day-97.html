Situation Summary

The defining feature of June 4 was a tentative turn toward de-escalation that events on the ground immediately undercut. The United States announced that Israel and Lebanon had agreed to implement a ceasefire — an end to Hezbollah fire, a withdrawal south of the Litani, and security zones under exclusive Lebanese control, with negotiations to resume later in the month — but the truce was tested before it could take hold: Israeli strikes continued in southern Lebanon, and Hezbollah rejected the latest agreement. The kinetic tempo against U.S. forces, by contrast, eased. CENTCOM said it intercepted “an additional wave of Iranian drones” aimed at American forces in Kuwait, with no Americans or assets harmed; Iran’s navy claimed it had struck a U.S. CENTCOM facility “responsible for recent U.S. actions,” a claim CENTCOM denied. Crucially, no new U.S. strike on Iranian territory was reported — a marked contrast to the June 2–3 Qeshm Island strikes and the Hellfire tanker interdiction that had defined the prior two days.

Diplomatically, the window closed where it opened: stuck. Iran’s Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the talks had made “no progress” even as he insisted the channels remained open, and he defended the attacks on Gulf states as self-defense; chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf warned that Tehran could abandon the talks for “confrontation” if Israeli strikes on Lebanon continued. President Trump kept up his more optimistic framing — a day earlier he had said a deal “could happen over the weekend” — but the tentative sixty-day extension framework reached May 24–28 remained unsigned, with Iran’s amendments on enriched-uranium disposal, the Hormuz language, and access to frozen funds still unresolved. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent testified before the House on department priorities, and the macroeconomic backdrop softened: U.S. weekly jobless claims hit their highest level since the war began.

Markets read the day as de-escalation. Oil eased on the Lebanon-ceasefire hopes — Brent slipped about 1.1 percent intraday toward $96.70 and WTI about 0.9 percent toward $95.17, unwinding part of the early-June Gulf-escalation premium — and AAA national gasoline edged down to $4.241. U.S. equities were mixed in a textbook value rotation: the S&P 500 recovered 0.41 percent to 7,584.31, a day after snapping its nine-session record streak, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average surged 1.73 percent to a record close of 51,561.93, led by UnitedHealth, JPMorgan, and Walmart, as money rotated out of semiconductors after Broadcom’s roughly 15 percent post-earnings drop; the Nasdaq slipped 0.09 percent. On the humanitarian front, an Israeli drone strike on a vehicle on the Zefta–Kfarwa Road in southern Lebanon wounded several, and at least nine Palestinians were killed in Israeli air raids overnight; the Lebanese Ministry of Public Health’s cumulative toll held near 3,473, with no confirmed new Lebanese fatalities in the window. The war reaches its hundredth day on June 7.

Ceasefire Under Fire: An Unsigned MoU and Near-Daily Strikes

“Ceasefire-under-fire” is now the war’s structural baseline. A tentative sixty-day extension framework was reached May 24–28 — mediated through Pakistan, with a separate thirty-to-ninety-day nuclear track — yet the memorandum of understanding remains unsigned, and both sides exchanged strikes around the Strait of Hormuz on June 3 while each insisted the truce still held. Vice President Vance framed the doctrine plainly: the ceasefire “remains in place but the U.S. reserves the right to launch defensive strikes.” Defense Secretary Hegseth supplied the window’s defining line — “for the most part, ceasefire means fire is ceasing” — an almost candid admission that “ceasefire” now describes a managed exchange rather than a halt. No side has formally declared the truce over.

The day’s kinetics were the heaviest in weeks: an Iranian strike on Kuwait International Airport that killed an Indian national and wounded more than sixty, U.S. self-defense strikes on a Qeshm Island ground-control station and an Iranian oil tanker near Hormuz, and intercepted Iranian missile and drone salvos against Kuwait and Bahrain. The diplomatic signals were contradictory. Secretary of State Rubio told congressional hearings that sanctions relief is tied strictly to nuclear concessions, not the Strait of Hormuz, and dismissed Iranian state-media accounts of a Hormuz-for-sanctions bargain as never offered. President Trump, on a podcast, said Iran had already agreed not to build a nuclear weapon and that the MoU was still being negotiated with Iranian amendments outstanding — the unresolved items reportedly covering enriched-uranium disposal, the Hormuz language, and access to frozen funds.

Lebanon is the decoupling wildcard with leverage out of proportion to its formal scope. Iran insists any wider deal cover Lebanon; Netanyahu keeps the tracks separate and continues striking Hezbollah ahead of fall elections. The reported Trump–Netanyahu friction — Trump confirmed he had called the Israeli prime minister “crazy” over Lebanon — is the single most volatile variable, and an Israeli escalation there could collapse the U.S.–Iran track outright. The cleanest near-term test is Trump’s own framing that the MoU could be signed within “the week”: a signature would be a genuine de-escalation signal, while another failed week keeps the low-intensity-exchange pattern intact.

Kuwait Airport Strike, Qeshm, and the Hormuz Tanker Exchange

The window’s deadliest event was Iran’s strike on Kuwait International Airport. An Iranian drone-and-missile attack on June 3 killed one Indian national and wounded more than sixty, heavily damaging a terminal and nearby diplomatic missions and forcing a brief airport closure before Kuwait Airways and Jazeera Airways resumed operations from a different terminal the same day. Kuwait’s Defense Ministry said it destroyed more than a dozen missiles and roughly a dozen drones, and Kuwait expelled two Iranian diplomats. In a now-familiar pattern, the accounts diverged: the IRGC denied striking the airport at all, blaming failed U.S. interceptors, while CENTCOM said two Iranian missiles fell short or broke up and multiple drones were downed.

The U.S. strikes that Iran cast as the provocation were two. Over June 2–3, CENTCOM hit an Iranian military ground-control station on Qeshm Island; on June 3 it fired an AGM-114 Hellfire at an Iranian oil tanker near the strait that it said was attempting to breach the blockade, damaging the vessel’s engine room per the IRGC and Tasnim. The IRGC said it retaliated by targeting a vessel it named “Panaya” and warned that “disrupting the security of the Strait of Hormuz will carry a heavy price for the U.S. military.” A parallel Iranian salvo at Bahrain was intercepted — Bahrain’s army downed three missiles and several drones — even as the IRGC claimed strikes on the U.S. Fifth Fleet headquarters, an airbase, and helicopters that CENTCOM dismissed as false. Domestic Rebuke: The House Passes a War-Powers Resolution 215–208

For the first time, a chamber of Congress formally voted to rein in the war. The U.S. House passed an Iran war-powers resolution 215–208 on June 3 — the first such measure to clear the House since the conflict began — with four Republicans, Representatives Thomas Massie, Brian Fitzpatrick, Tom Barrett, and Warren Davidson, crossing party lines to join a unified Democratic caucus. The resolution is largely symbolic: it now goes to the Senate, and a presidential veto remains the administration’s backstop. But the vote is the clearest institutional signal yet that the political coalition behind Operation Epic Fury is fraying.

The numbers around the vote sharpen the point. A PBS/NPR/Marist poll released the same week found that 60 percent of Americans disapprove of President Trump’s handling of the conflict — a figure that, paired with four Republican defections, signals slipping support within the president’s own party. The threshold that would convert symbolism into genuine constraint is a Senate floor vote: that chamber’s version of the resolution is understood to carry more procedural force, and earlier Senate war-powers attempts have repeatedly failed by a handful of votes. A successful Senate vote would not by itself end the war — the veto pen still sits at the White House — but it would mark a qualitative shift in the domestic balance.

The timing compounds the pressure. The war reaches its hundredth day this week, with Day 100 falling on June 7, and the administration is managing a contested ceasefire, an unsigned MoU, and a Lebanon track that the president has publicly clashed with Israel over — all while a majority of the public has turned against the campaign. The next markers to watch on the domestic axis are whether the Senate schedules its own floor vote and whether further Republicans break; on the conflict axis, whether the MoU is signed within Trump’s stated “next week” window or the low-intensity exchanges that defined June 3 harden into something worse.
