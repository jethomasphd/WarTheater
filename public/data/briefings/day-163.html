Situation Summary

For the first time since the ceasefire collapsed on July 7, a pause has held. No American munitions have fallen on Iranian soil since the July 29 heavy wave — a full week of quiet through August 9, the longest stretch of the renewed war. But the guns did not fall silent so much as change hands. The kinetic energy migrated from US strikes on Iran to Iranian and proxy attacks on shipping: an uncrewed surface vessel sank a ship off Al Mukha in the Red Sea on August 4, Yemen’s Houthis claimed a Saudi-aligned tanker the next day, and on August 8 an Iranian missile struck a UAE/ADNOC oil tanker in the Strait of Hormuz and left it burning. The pause is real; the war is not over.

The diplomacy the pause was meant to enable has hardened rather than converged. On August 8 Iran’s Supreme National Security Council published formal preconditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz — lift the US naval blockade, withdraw American forces, permanently end the war, and, per Fortune, pay war-damage compensation — and its parliament weighed a draft markets read as stricter than expected, with a penalty equal to 20 percent of a vessel’s cargo value and full reopening only after the blockade lifts. Tehran called Trump’s diplomacy “theater.” Yet the structural signal runs the other way: the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Dan Caine, has privately told other advisers that Washington needs an off-ramp; Vice President Vance described talks over a safe-traffic system and demining and called the US “in the middle of the game”; and the two sides are passing messages through intermediaries. Iran is raising its price precisely as its adversary looks for the exit. Oman, the mediator, calls its talks “positive and constructive” but warns the tanker attacks threaten them — the clearest fragility indicator for the next cycle.

The financial story decoupled from the war this week, and that is the most telling development on the board. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757.64 on August 7, up 3.6 percent on the week and its strongest run since April — driven not by Iran but by a soft July jobs report, in which the economy unexpectedly shed 23,000 positions and took a September Fed rate hike off the table. Brent, meanwhile, fell more than 7 percent on the week to about $83.55 even as the tanker attacks kept a floor under it. The war premium is unwinding in oil while equities trade on the Fed; if the pause breaks, expect crude to re-spike faster than stocks re-rate. The human ledger barely moved: the one firm revision was US wounded, raised from 624 to 687 on an August 4 Pentagon DCAS read, while every cumulative Iranian, Lebanese, displacement and flight figure is held and stale. And the war’s newest casualties — the seafarers on fifteen ADNOC vessels attacked to date, one killed and twenty injured — fall into a category this dashboard’s five national-toll lines structurally miss.

The Pause Holds, and the Fighting Moves to the Water

Three previous escalate-then-pause cycles since June each broke within days. This one has not. No American munitions have struck Iranian soil since the July 29 heavy wave, and by August 9 the quiet had run a full week — the longest lull since the ceasefire collapsed on July 7. What makes it more than a lull is where the de-escalation happened: the strike Trump called off on August 1 was aimed at Iranian energy infrastructure, the target set both sides treat as the line between a war over shipping and a war over the Iranian economy. Standing that down, and then not re-tasking it for eight days, is a qualitatively different signal than the tactical pauses that preceded it.

But the guns did not go silent; they changed hands and moved offshore. An uncrewed surface vessel struck a ship off Al Mukha in the Red Sea on August 4, and it sank. Yemen’s Houthis claimed a Saudi-aligned tanker the next day. And on August 8 an Iranian missile hit a UAE/ADNOC oil tanker in the Strait of Hormuz — the fifteenth ADNOC vessel attacked in the war — and left it burning in what the IRGC-linked Sepah News called the “US-supported shipping route.” The kinetic energy of the war has migrated from US strikes on Iran to Iranian and proxy attacks on shipping, which is precisely why oil stays bid even as equities price peace.

The one constant across strikes and stand-down alike is the blockade. CENTCOM said on August 8 it had now redirected 51 commercial vessels and disabled two under the renewed regime, up from 35 a week earlier — the chokehold tightening while the shooting paused. That is the asymmetry to hold onto: the United States is squeezing Iran economically without firing, Iran is answering at sea rather than at the source, and the corridor the US is trying to protect is the one taking the hits. Oman, brokering between them, warned that these vessel attacks threaten its talks. They are the fragility indicator for whether week two of the pause becomes week three.

Iran Raises Its Price as Washington Looks for the Door

Iran spent the week hardening its terms. On August 8 the secretary of its Supreme National Security Council, Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, published formal preconditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz: lift the US naval blockade, withdraw American forces, permanently end the war, and — per Fortune — pay war-damage compensation. Two days earlier the parliament had reviewed a draft that markets read as stricter than expected, attaching a penalty equal to 20 percent of a vessel’s cargo value and making full reopening conditional on the blockade lifting first. Tehran called Trump’s diplomacy “theater” and, as it has all along, denied any direct talks. The strait is Iran’s remaining leverage, and it is pricing it accordingly.

Washington moved the other way. The Chairman of the Joint Chiefs, General Dan Caine, has privately told other top advisers that the United States needs to find an off-ramp from the war, according to three sources — a remarkable signal from the officer running it. Vice President Vance, describing the negotiation publicly, said it centered on a safe-traffic system, demining and an Iranian commitment not to attack vessels, and called the US “in the middle of the game.” The two governments are passing messages through intermediaries rather than sitting across a table. The structural picture is a negotiation in which one side raises its demands precisely as the other searches for the exit — a configuration that can resolve quickly if a mediator bridges it, or stall indefinitely if neither will move first.

Oman is that mediator, and its read is the one to watch. It called its talks “positive and constructive” while warning that the vessel attacks threaten them, and an Iran–Oman framework for managing Strait traffic was reported near final — though even a signed framework would not automatically reopen the waterway. Read Iran’s denials carefully: dismissing a characterization of terms is not the same as refusing to talk, and mediated processes in this war have repeatedly been denied in public while proceeding in private. The reported roughly sixty-day negotiating clock from the collapsed interim understanding is said to run down in little more than a week. Whether it is extended, and whether the US ties any blockade relief to the Oman framework, will define the next cycle.

An Eight Percent Round Trip on a Sentence: What Oil Is Actually Pricing

Follow the barrel through the week and the war almost disappears from it. Brent came out of July 27 at $88.36, drifted lower on July 28 as the market read de-escalation, then jumped 7.9 percent to settle $90.74 on July 29 — its biggest daily gain in more than two weeks — after the overnight Iranian attack and the American heavy wave. It gave back about two percent to $89.03 on July 30, not on any military development but on Saudi Arabia’s naval-coalition proposal, and closed the month at $90.12 on July 31, capping a July gain of roughly 20 percent, Brent’s best month since March. Then on August 3, with no strike, no interception and no change in physical supply, it slumped to about $83 because Trump said negotiations would begin. WTI ran the same path to about $79.

That last move is the analytically important one. An eight percent round trip from the July 29 settle, executed on an announcement rather than a barrel, is a direct measurement of how much of the current price is war premium rather than physical shortage. It makes the oil panel the single most market-moving variable on this dashboard and a leading indicator rather than a lagging one: a signed text plus verified sustained Hormuz transits would likely break Brent below $80, while a collapse of talks re-prices the premium back toward $90 and above. Note also that Brent rolled from the September to the October contract at month-end, so the August 3 quote is not contract-continuous with the July series, and that the August 3 figures here are intraday and provisional pending official settles.

The physical picture has not yet ratified the optimism, and that gap is where the risk sits. Hormuz carried about 14 commercial ships in 24 hours around July 28–29 and roughly 12 on August 3 — five inbound, seven outbound, six of them running dark, per Windward — against more than a hundred a day before the war. A waterway that normally moves about 20 million barrels a day of crude and products, a quarter of seaborne oil trade, was already down about 30 percent year-on-year in the first quarter at 14.6 million barrels a day. The IRGC’s July 31 claim to have hit two escorted tankers and turned four back remains unconfirmed by US and UK maritime authorities, with Kpler still logging two VLCCs out of the Gulf that day — but unverified claims still price into war-risk cover, which is its own transmission channel. Equities, meanwhile, took the harder hit this week than crude did: down 1.52 percent on July 29 in the worst Dow session since April 2025, with the Fed holding rates amid oil-driven inflation fears and the 30-year yield at a two-decade high. Higher long yields raise the carrying cost of the very supplemental that funds the war.

A Deal With One Signatory, and a Public That Has Stopped Buying

The framework Trump described on August 1 is, at present, a claim made by one party about an agreement the other party denies. Its stated terms are consequential: the “Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT” alongside an end to Iran’s nuclear threat — which would resolve the two issues that have driven the entire war. He said Iran and other Middle Eastern countries had asked him to hold off on the strike, and Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman is reported to have urged restraint. Against that, Mehr called the account “simply a new lie” on August 2, the defense minister said Iran was “neither surprised nor passive” and remained on alert, and Tehran maintains there are no direct US–Iran negotiations. Trump nonetheless said on August 3 that talks begin, framing it as “a negotiation” and telling his own side to “get to work.” Read the denial carefully: dismissing a characterization of terms is not the same as refusing to talk, and mediated processes in this war have repeatedly been denied publicly while proceeding privately. But nothing here is verified.

The domestic ground has shifted under the policy. An AP-NORC poll conducted July 23–27 — 1,165 adults, margin of error 3.7 points — found 64 percent of American adults saying the war is not worth the risks against 33 percent saying it is, with Trump’s approval on his handling of Iran at 28 percent. Those numbers were taken before this window’s escalation, not after, which means they reflect the war as it stood during a pause rather than after a fresh American strike wave. Set against Moody’s estimate of roughly a thousand dollars per typical US household and counting, the polling reads as the political price of an economic one, and it is a plausible part of the explanation for why a strike package was assembled and then not flown.

Congress remains on a separate clock. The $87.6 billion supplemental, $67.1 billion of it for the Pentagon, is still pending with no confirmed markup or vote date, alongside the House-passed $95 billion blueprint and a Pentagon continuing resolution through December 4. Official direct US military cost stands unchanged at $37.5 billion — Defense Secretary Hegseth’s July 21 figure to Senate Appropriations — with CBS sources noting the real number is higher because it excludes reconstruction of damaged American bases. Across 157 days that is a blended $239 million a day, which is not the same measure as the $500 million active-operations rate this dashboard tracks and should never be conflated with it. Washington is still financing a long war while its president announces the end of one.

What to Watch

  • Whether talks convene at all, and in what format: Trump said negotiations begin Monday; Iran denies direct talks and called the framework “a new lie.” Watch for the first artifact either side cannot deny — a venue, a mediator statement, a named delegation. Until then this is an announcement, not a process, and the burden of proof sits with the claim.
  • Hormuz transit counts as the only real verification: The deal’s headline term is a “Complete and Total” opening of the Strait. Traffic ran about 12 on August 3 — six of them dark — against more than 100 pre-war. Sustained transits in the dozens would confirm the framework is real; flat counts would mean nothing has changed regardless of what is said.
  • Brent below $80 or back above $90 as the fork: Crude executed an eight percent round trip on an announcement alone. A break below $80 would price a durable deal; a return above $90 would mean the market has stopped believing it. Treat the oil panel as the fastest available referendum on the talks.
  • Whether the canceled energy-infrastructure strike is re-tasked: The package was assembled and stood down, not abandoned, with forces “locked and loaded.” A strike on Iranian refineries or export terminals would be the largest single escalation of the war and would reset every financial series on this board. It remains the loaded gun on the table.
  • How far Saudi Arabia goes: Riyadh joined US strikes publicly on July 28 and convened 40 nations on a naval coalition on July 30, while reportedly urging restraint on August 1. Watch whether the coalition produces actual escort commitments, and whether Saudi participation in strikes recurs — Gulf-state belligerence is a structural change worth its own tracker.
  • A relief-in-place for the Lincoln: The carrier is unrelieved past 207 consecutive days at sea, a record and a readiness liability compounding weekly. A relief would itself be a major naval-disposition event; its continued absence is a signal about how thin forward presence has become. No August 3 USNI tracker was available for this cycle — re-verify.
  • Whether DCAS reconciles 624 against 642: The Pentagon closed the Epic Fury tally and opened an “Overseas Operations” category, producing two official wounded totals for the same war. Watch for a merge, a restatement, or a third discontinuity — and treat any single unqualified casualty figure with suspicion until one arrives.
  • Fresh humanitarian sourcing, or continued silence: Lebanon’s toll, displacement, flight cancellations and Iranian child casualties are all weeks to months stale, with no in-window statements located. A Lebanese Ministry of Public Health or UN OCHA release would be the single most valuable data recovery available next cycle.
