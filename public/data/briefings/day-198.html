Situation Summary

For the first time in this war, the sharpest week of escalation in more than a month produced almost no deaths. Between September 7 and 13 the United States destroyed five Iranian oil tankers, the IRGC put roughly twenty ballistic missiles into a US air base in Jordan, drones shut Saudi Arabia’s main pipeline bypass, and Iran-aligned Houthis seized control of the Bab el-Mandeb strait — and the tracked national casualty lines stayed flat, because the fighting had moved off people and onto logistics. The one confirmed death of the window came on the last day, when an unidentified projectile killed a seafarer aboard an Iranian merchant vessel near Hengam Island. The war’s kinetic load now falls on tankers, pipelines and chokepoints, not populations.

That shift has a structural driver: for the first time, all three of the region’s primary crude arteries are degraded at once. The Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed; on September 10–11 drones launched from Iraq forced Saudi Arabia to shut its 1,200-kilometre East-West (Petroline) pipeline, the kingdom’s principal Hormuz bypass to the Red Sea; and by September 11 the Houthis had taken Mokha, the town of Dhubab and Mayun (Perim) Island, seizing physical control of Bab el-Mandeb. The result was the most violent repricing of crude since the spring: Brent settled at $107.63 on September 10, its highest close since May, WTI cleared $100, and US diesel topped $6 a gallon for the first time on record. Only a pullback on Hormuz-diplomacy headlines pulled Brent back to about $104 by Friday.

Diplomacy and escalation are now running on the same clock. Parliament speaker Ghalibaf’s declaration that “the era of proportionate responses is over” and Vice President Vance’s no-talks line coexist with a first-ever GCC–Iran foreign-ministers’ meeting, set for Salalah, Oman on September 14 to weigh a temporary Hormuz transit arrangement, and with President Trump’s softer suggestion that talks are possible and the war may end after the midterm election. Equities, once again, looked through all of it: the S&P 500 snapped a four-day losing streak on September 11 on an in-line August CPI print, closing at 7,656.98 with the VIX under 16. The war premium still lives in the energy complex, not the stock market.

The Strike Pause Collapses: US and Iran Resume Exchanging Fire

The defining act of the week was a strike, not a sanction. The pause on Iranian soil that had held since roughly August 1 broke over the weekend of August 30–31, when the IRGC attempted to lay sea mines in the Strait of Hormuz. CENTCOM struck the rocket launchers staged to fire them — the first US strike on Iran-associated targets in about a month — and Iran retaliated with eight ballistic missiles at a US base in Jordan, all intercepted. By September 1 CENTCOM had formally announced it was resuming strikes on IRGC targets to protect Hormuz shipping and US forces, and Trump confirmed the US was “striking Iranian Targets near the Strait of Hormuz.”

The resumption was immediately costly for civilians. A US wave on southern and western Iran on September 1 killed at least eighteen and wounded 142, according to Iran’s Health Ministry, with a component of at least seven killed in Khuzestan (four of them IRGC Aerospace personnel, per regional agencies). The most contested strike hit a wedding in Kuhestak, in Sirik County: Iranian state media said a missile came through the roof of the room where the party was underway, killing at least five — including a four-year-old boy and a sixteen-year-old — and wounding sixty to seventy, most of them women and children. CENTCOM would neither confirm nor deny a strike at the location, insisting “the US military never targets civilians,” and Vice President Vance said he was “extremely skeptical” a US strike had hit a wedding. The figures are Iranian, attributed and unverified, and the dashboard’s aggregate Iranian-dead line is deliberately held rather than synthesized.

Iran’s answer on September 2 was the most expansive round since the fighting resumed. Jordan confirmed thirteen missiles entered its airspace targeting the King Hussein and Al-Azraq air bases, with three impacts in remote areas and no damage; Iran also claimed the Marine training facility at Camp Titin and Muwaffaq Salti Air Base. Kuwait and Bahrain’s Shaikh Isa Air Base reported attacks with no casualties, and Kurdish authorities intercepted ten explosive drones near Erbil. The IRGC claimed — without confirmation, and inconsistent with an unchanged DCAS death toll of eighteen — that it had killed “a large number of” US troops at Camp Titin and Shaikh Isa and downed an MQ-9. What had been a sanctions-and-diplomacy standoff a week earlier was again an open, two-sided kinetic exchange.

Hormuz Becomes a Live Combat Zone: The ‘Tanker for Tanker’ War

The Strait of Hormuz is no longer a tripwire — it is the battlefield. Beginning September 2, the United States opened a maritime-economic campaign distinct from its land strikes, hitting two Iranian government oil tankers in their engine rooms while at anchor under what officials called a “tanker for tanker” doctrine. It is the clearest sign yet that Washington intends to fight this phase against Iran’s export fleet rather than only its coastline, blurring the line between the kinetic war and the Treasury “Economic Outcast” sanctions campaign that defined late August.

The doctrine was tested at its most dangerous on September 5. The IRGC launched ballistic missiles at a US aircraft carrier and a destroyer in the Gulf of Oman area; both missed. CENTCOM called the apparent targeting of a carrier “a notable provocation” and retaliated by permanently disabling two Iranian oil tankers and destroying a third, including the M/T Kylo, also known as the “Noxen.” Admiral Brad Cooper articulated the escalating economic logic: “If you shoot at two of our ships, we will impose an even higher economic cost — taking out three of yours… destroy Iran’s limited and exposed oil fleet.” The IRGC Navy, for its part, claimed it had targeted three tankers on an “unauthorized route” plus three US-affiliated vessels, and warned all Gulf shipping.

The chokepoint stays effectively closed and now openly hostile. Transit counts remain far below normal — TradingEconomics recorded about six commodity vessels through the strait on Wednesday, September 2, down from eleven on Tuesday and against a ten-day average near thirteen. Which US carrier the IRGC targeted was not disclosed by CENTCOM or ABC, and it is not attributed to a named hull here pending confirmation; the file’s two on-station carriers, George Washington and George H.W. Bush, are flagged as event-driven, not repositioned. The single most important escalation question of the next cycle is whether a US combat death or an actual carrier hit follows — either would be the trigger to end the market’s decoupling in an instant.

Markets Stay Decoupled: The War Premium Lives in Crude

The most striking feature of the week is what did not happen: a US aircraft carrier was targeted by ballistic missiles, and equities barely moved. The VIX closed Thursday at 14.53 — a level associated with calm, not conflict — and the S&P 500 finished the week roughly flat at 7,718.60, up only about a tenth of a percent. The index actually rose on Wednesday and Thursday before slipping 0.38 percent on Friday, and that decline was a macro story, not a war story: a hotter-than-expected August jobs report (162,000 against about 55,000 expected, unemployment 4.1 percent) pushed the odds of a September Fed rate hike to roughly 58 percent and lifted two-year Treasury yields to their highest since January 2025.

The war premium has migrated almost entirely into crude. Oil posted its strongest week since mid-July: Brent traded near $96.28 and WTI near $91.48 by September 4, both up roughly eight to ten percent on the week, reversing the late-August “sanctions story” compression as strikes resumed and Hormuz risk returned. Oilprice.com pegged October WTI up 10.02 percent for the week; only the September 4 marks are firm, with the intraweek settles provisional. A missile in the strait is a supply shock in a way that slow-acting sanctions are not, and the crude complex repriced accordingly even as stocks looked through it.

At the pump, the pass-through lags. AAA’s national average sat near $4.15, up only about six cents on the week despite the crude spike — the usual two-to-three-week retail lag, with the seasonal switch to cheaper winter-blend gasoline approaching. That leaves gasoline up about $1.16, or nearly 39 percent, against the $2.99 pre-war baseline, well below the May 21 peak of $4.55. If Brent holds near $96, the pump is the variable most likely to catch up over the next one to two weeks — the delayed civilian cost of a war the markets have otherwise tuned out.

The Escalation Ceiling Rises: A Carrier Targeted, Diplomacy Inverted

Two things this week were genuine step-changes. The first was physical: the IRGC fired ballistic missiles at a US aircraft carrier, an act of a different order from strikes on land bases or shipping. The second was rhetorical: on September 6, Iran’s chief negotiator and parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf declared that “the era of proportionate responses is over,” calling the week’s strikes on US bases “only the beginning” and warning of responses “faster, heavier, and more painful.” Together they lift the ceiling on how far this can go, and they make the identity of the targeted carrier and any first US combat death of the resumed phase the metrics to watch.

Diplomacy did not just stall; it inverted. Late August had produced an Iran–Oman Strait of Hormuz revenue-sharing framework and Qatari mediation in Tehran. By September 3, Vice President Vance had ruled out any talks unless Iran first stops attacking commercial shipping in the strait, and pointedly declined to call the conflict a war. Israel’s defense minister, meanwhile, threatened “crippling” strikes on Iran’s military and energy infrastructure — a threat that, if executed, would be the fastest route from the current standoff to a genuine oil supply shock. Whether the June memorandum of understanding is now formally dead is an open question the next cycle should answer.

The economic-cost doctrine at sea is the connective tissue between the war’s kinetic and financial fronts. By striking Iran’s “shadow fleet” — five tankers in a week — Washington is using ordnance to do what secondary sanctions do slowly, and Admiral Cooper’s framing suggests a deliberate ladder of escalating cost rather than a one-off. The risk is symmetry: an American campaign against Iranian tankers invites a broader IRGC campaign against Gulf shipping, and the IRGC Navy’s warning to “all” vessels on “unauthorized” routes points exactly that way. The war has found a new center of gravity, and it floats.
