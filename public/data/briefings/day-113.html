The Signed Memorandum: From Versailles to a Cancelled Switzerland Ceremony

The defining event of the window was the signing itself. The fourteen-point U.S.–Iran memorandum was signed on June 17 — by President Trump at the Palace of Versailles, after the G7 at Évian-les-Bains, and by Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian in Tehran. The June 19 Bürgenstock event near Lucerne was a separate follow-on technical-talks ceremony, and it was cancelled: the Swiss foreign ministry said it “would not proceed,” Vice President Vance no longer travelled (citing logistics), Pakistan’s prime minister pulled out, and Iran declined, insisting the Lebanon fighting stop first. The agreement is a framework — the “Islamabad Memorandum” — not a treaty: it ends the war on all fronts including Lebanon and opens a sixty-day window, started June 18, toward a final, U.N.-endorsed deal that either side may abandon until it is binding.

The mechanics that took effect immediately are narrow but real. CENTCOM lifted the naval blockade on June 18 and the Strait of Hormuz reopened toll-free for sixty days, with Iran’s “Persian Gulf Strait Authority” suspending its charges; Treasury was to issue crude and petrochemical export waivers at once. The larger prizes are back-loaded to a final deal: the termination of U.N., IAEA-board and unilateral U.S. sanctions on an agreed schedule, a reconstruction-and-development plan of at least $300 billion arranged with regional partners, and the release of frozen Iranian assets (Iranian state media cited $24 billion over the talks). On the nuclear file, Iran reaffirmed it will never build a weapon, and its 60-percent-enriched stockpile — the last public IAEA figure was 440.9 kilograms, unverified since Iran barred inspectors from bombed sites — is to be down-blended on Iranian soil under IAEA supervision.

The walk-backs were immediate. Beyond the cancelled Switzerland ceremony, Iran’s National Security Council said it retains a “reciprocal response” plan should Washington violate the deal; chief negotiator Mohammad Baqer Qalibaf said the talks remain bound by Tehran’s “red lines”; and new Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei approved the memorandum in writing only while warning against U.S. “excessive demands.” Tehran had not publicly confirmed the U.S. wording of the fourteen points as of the window, and Iranian state-media versions differ on details such as the size of the asset release and the blockade-withdrawal phrasing — reminders that the document is an opening position, not a settled peace.

Markets and Oil: Peace Priced In

Equities whipsawed around the signing before settling into a peace footing. June 17 brought a broad sell-off — the S&P 500 fell 1.21 percent to 7,420.10 and the Dow shed 507 points to 51,492.55 — driven not by the war but by a hawkish June decision from the Federal Reserve under its new chair, Kevin Warsh. The next session reversed it: on June 18 the S&P rose 1.08 percent to 7,500.58 in a tech-led rebound (Intel jumped about ten percent on an Apple chip deal, with Micron and Nvidia firmer) as CENTCOM lifted the blockade. U.S. markets were then closed June 19 for Juneteenth, and Trump touted a “record high” tape and “tumbling” oil.

Oil told the cleaner story: the war premium is nearly gone. Brent settled around $79.55 on June 17, $79.85 on June 18 and $80.57 on June 19 — the last a 0.9-percent gain after Switzerland cancelled its talks — while WTI settled $76.60 on June 18 and traded near $77.54 intraday on June 19, with no clean settle published. Crude was still on track for an eight-to-ten-percent weekly decline, having erased most of the gains from peaks above $110 (briefly near $130 at the war’s outset). Source variance is worth flagging: Oilprice.com showed Brent near $78.95 against Trading Economics’ $80.57 around June 19.

Consumer relief is finally arriving, with a lag. AAA’s national gasoline average dipped to $3.973 on June 19, its first sustained move below $4.00 since late March, though daily readings vary by source (a Finder/AAA figure was about $4.05 on June 18). The cumulative bill remains large and scope-dependent: Moody’s Analytics pegs the cost to U.S. consumers and taxpayers at about $132 billion, well above the Pentagon’s $29 billion official operational figure (with a roughly $80 billion supplemental request pending) and a ~$34.7 billion phased military tracker. The World Bank, in its June 11 outlook, cut 2026 global growth to 2.5 percent — its lowest since the pandemic’s onset — and slashed Gulf economies’ growth to 1.3 percent, a reminder that the war’s macro shadow outlasts its kinetics.

Hormuz: Blockade Lifted, the Strait Reopens Toll-Free

The strait, the deal’s centerpiece, actually moved. On June 18 CENTCOM announced that “all U.S. military blockade enforcement efforts have ceased,” and the Strait of Hormuz reopened toll-free for the memorandum’s sixty-day window. Shipping resumed for the first time in roughly 110 days: Lloyd’s List Intelligence logged major owners — Grimaldi, Cosco, Knutsen and NYK — transiting, and Vance said more than 12.5 million barrels passed on Wednesday night and about 10 million more on Thursday, including the first Saudi-owned tankers since the war began. Iran’s “Persian Gulf Strait Authority” suspended its wartime tolls for the duration of the window.

The scale of what is unwinding is large. The EIA puts pre-war Hormuz oil flow at roughly 20 million barrels a day in 2024 — about a fifth of global petroleum-liquids consumption — and reported that first-quarter 2026 flows had already fallen to 14.6 million barrels a day, down about 6 million year-on-year as the war bit. But the reopening is not yet a flood: by Friday morning, amid the Switzerland cancellation and Lebanon’s deadliest post-MOU day, no outbound vessels were seen leaving the Persian Gulf, and Iran signalled that transiting ships would eventually need mandatory war-risk insurance — free for now, possibly charged later.

Two caveats temper the picture. Mine-neutralization under the memorandum — Iran is to restore pre-war merchant traffic within thirty days — has not been independently verified, and the Joint Maritime Information Center is still advising transiting vessels to hug the Omani coast to reduce residual mine risk. And the naval enforcement architecture has not gone home: CENTCOM said its warships “will remain in the general area to make sure that all aspects of the agreement are adhered to,” and USNI’s last published tracker (June 15) places both the USS Abraham Lincoln and the USS George H.W. Bush in the Arabian Sea, with no carrier withdrawal announced. The benchmark for a genuine wind-down would be a carrier departing the theater — which has not happened.

Lebanon: The Residual Hot War and the Deal’s Fault Line

With the Iran front quiet, Lebanon became the war’s only moving front — and on June 19 it produced the deadliest day since the memorandum. Hezbollah struck an Israeli tank near the Ali al-Taher hilltop overlooking Nabatieh, killing four IDF soldiers, and said it was responding to Israeli ceasefire violations. Israel struck “Hezbollah infrastructure” in Nabatieh, the Bekaa Valley town of Douris and elsewhere, and the Lebanese Health Ministry reported at least forty-seven killed (an earlier overnight count was eighteen). Netanyahu ordered the IDF to strike “with full force.”

That escalation was pulled back within hours. Israel and Hezbollah renewed their ceasefire around 9 a.m. Eastern on June 19, mediated by the United States, Qatar and Iran; Trump said he had phoned Israeli officials to request it, calling the truce “a little icing on the cake,” with Israel and Lebanon expected to meet in Washington the following week. The calm did not hold cleanly: on June 20, Israeli strikes on Nabatieh-area towns killed at least seven, including two children, after Hezbollah reportedly fired more than fifty projectiles overnight — testing a truce barely a day old.

The structural weakness is unchanged and decisive. The memorandum binds the war to end on “all fronts, including Lebanon,” yet Israel and Hezbollah are not signatories: Iran treats continued Israeli operations as violations, the United States says the deal does not require an Israeli withdrawal, and Israeli officials say it does not bind them at all. That gap — a front no signatory fully controls, written into a deal between Washington and Tehran — is the fault line most likely to collapse the agreement, and it has already generated both the deadliest post-MOU day and a same-day truce. Whether the renewed ceasefire survives is the clearest near-term test of whether the larger peace holds.
