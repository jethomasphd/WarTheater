Situation Summary

The fifteen days from October 16 to October 30 left the war running on two tracks that meet at the same date. No US or Israeli strike on Iranian territory was found in the window; AP reports the US has not struck Iran since early September, and the pre-midterm pledge holds until November 3. The strike track, though, is now concrete. US officials told the New York Times on October 21 that the US and Israel have plans for fresh strikes after the vote, and on October 28 the WSJ and AP reported that Washington had told Gulf and other allies to prepare for a short, intense campaign lasting perhaps several days, and for Iranian retaliation on energy infrastructure. The WSJ adds munitions restocking, a Patriot battery shipped from Texas to Israel and troops moved off exposed bases; the Theodore Roosevelt carrier group and Makin Island amphibious group are reportedly Middle East-bound. The diplomatic track is live too. On October 25 Qatar delivered a draft deal and a letter from Vice President Vance to SNSC Secretary Mohsen Rezaei proposing direct talks with the nuclear file on the table; a Qatari delegation was back in Tehran on October 30 to collect a reply that Iran has not yet formally given.

At sea, October became the worst month for attacks on Hormuz-area shipping since records began in 2017 (Reuters), with UKMTO counting 23 attacks since October 1. The tactics are diversifying toward deniable, close-range methods. Unknown projectiles struck the Kuwaiti tanker Al Dasma off Oman on October 21 and disabled an outbound tanker on October 23. Cooper says it has halted about $25 billion in Iranian crude exports and CENTCOM counted 138 vessels redirected by October 17. It is biting less on weapons: a Reuters investigation found Iran imported at least $2 billion in drone and missile components through some 5,000 military agencies since 2025.

Debris from an intercepted drone killed two foreign workers at the Shaybah oilfield and an attack on Dammam’s airport killed two more on October 21; Aramco evacuated its Dhahran headquarters on October 22 and the US ordered some diplomats’ families out of the kingdom. Two Tracks to November 3: Strike Plans and the Vance Letter

Two weeks ago the post-midterm strike option was a matter of reported planning. It is now a matter of logistics. On October 28 the WSJ and AP reported that the US had told Gulf and other allies to prepare for renewed strikes after November 3, described as a short, intense campaign of perhaps several days, and for Iranian retaliation against energy infrastructure. The WSJ reports munitions restocking, a Patriot battery shipped from Texas to Israel and troops moved off vulnerable bases. Ambassador Tom Barrack said on October 23 that a weekend strike was “absolutely” possible; Trump said he knew nothing of it and none came. On October 29 Hegseth fired the Joint Staff’s chief strategist, Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia, whom a Pentagon official accused of leaking classified material about the war. DNI Jay Clayton warned in an October 23 memo that Iran has substantially increased efforts to target US personnel, and on October 27 two drones, assessed by CTP-ISW as militia surveillance, probed the US Embassy in Baghdad.

The diplomatic track runs in parallel. Qatar delivered a draft deal and the Vance letter to Rezaei on October 25, proposing direct talks led by Vance with the nuclear file on the table from the start. Trump said he approved the contact; a senior Iranian official told Reuters the Qatari ideas were unacceptable, while Foreign Minister Araghchi said Iran could carry out its seven-day Hormuz plan even faster and Speaker Ghalibaf repeated that any deal requires US acceptance of Iran’s seven conditions. On October 30 a Qatari delegation returned to Tehran, reportedly to collect the answer, and officials floated talks in Doha or Istanbul as soon as next week. The two tracks are not alternatives so much as leverage on each other. Iran’s reply and the TR group’s transit of the Strait of Malacca are the two signals to read together. Israel adds uncertainty: its October 27 election left no bloc with a majority, and Netanyahu heads a caretaker government while coalition talks begin; the new Knesset is sworn in on November 10.

The Saudi-Houthi Front Becomes the Deadliest Theater

The Houthi target list has climbed from airports to the core of Saudi oil. The group claimed attacks on Aramco’s Yanbu site and the Shaybah field on October 19, neither confirmed. On October 21 debris from a drone intercepted over Shaybah killed two foreign workers, and an attack on Dammam’s King Fahd airport killed a Saudi citizen and an Indian resident and injured eight. Aramco evacuated its Dhahran headquarters on October 22. On October 27 the Houthis claimed their first strike on Ras Tanura, the kingdom’s largest refinery and a key export terminal; NASA FIRMS thermal data showed a fire at the terminal, but Saudi authorities have not confirmed a hit, so the dashboard carries it as unverified. Riyadh itself was targeted on October 20, 24, 25, 27 and 29, when at least eight explosions were heard as two ballistic missiles were downed. Missile debris injured two in Khamis Mushait on October 25, and on October 30 the coalition said it downed a drone over the Mecca region, which the Houthis denied launching.

Saudi air defense is becoming an international effort. The UK said on October 20 that RAF aircraft will intercept Houthi fire over the kingdom, and on October 26 Egypt’s parliament approved defensive military support, while Russia is reportedly supplying the Houthis with satellite imagery (CTP-ISW). Diplomacy has not slowed the attacks: Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman met Iranian leaders in Tehran on October 26 with no reported progress, and CTP-ISW ties the Ras Tanura escalation the next day to the failed talks. The war inside Yemen is intensifying as well. The Houthis hit Aden airport for the sixth time on October 28, and Houthi health authorities say Saudi-led strikes killed 14 at a market in al-Hazm that day and 12 in Taiz on October 29 (unverified by AP). OCHA counts more than 281,000 people displaced in Yemen since August. None of this enters the dashboard’s toll counters, which track the US-Iran-Israel-Lebanon war, so the most lethal theater of the window is largely invisible in the headline numbers.

War at Sea: Limpet Charges, Disputed Mines and a Six-Month High for Brent

October was the worst month for attacks on shipping in and around Hormuz since tracking began in 2017. UKMTO counts 23 attacks since October 1 and says owners leave most incidents unreported; Global Energy Flow’s UKMTO-confirmed floor is at least 15 vessels. The methods are shifting toward close, deniable attacks. A small boat reportedly fired an RPG at the tanker Loyalty on October 16, and unknown projectiles hit the Kuwaiti tanker Al Dasma off Bukha on October 21, an LPG carrier off Limah on October 22 and an outbound tanker, which was disabled, on October 23. On October 29 a small boat attached two explosive devices to the tanker Louise 1 25 nautical miles northeast of Khasab, starting an engine-room fire that briefly disabled it. Reuters sources call it the first known successful use of such mines in the war. Its attacker is unidentified, and the ship has shadow-fleet links (it was earlier hit by Ukrainian naval drones in the Black Sea). Iran’s claims are harder to test: the IRGC and Iran’s navy said four tankers on the US-promoted southern route hit mines on October 29 and three more were hit on October 30, and CENTCOM called the mine claims false. Iran’s blacklist of non-compliant ships has grown to 125, and it insists traffic use the northern routes it administers. The dashboard raises its attacked-vessel floor to about 93, counting only named or UKMTO-logged strikes.
