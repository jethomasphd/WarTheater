Situation Summary

The entire arc of this war ran once more inside seven days. The pause that closed Day 150 broke on July 28, when the IRGC fired ballistic missiles at American forces in the region at 5:45 p.m. Eastern — an “attempted surprise attack” that hit nothing, with Jordan alone downing five. President Trump, meeting Prime Minister Netanyahu at the White House that afternoon, vowed to “beat the f---ing s--- out of them.” He did: CENTCOM completed a “heavy wave” at 10 p.m. Eastern on July 29 against dozens of IRGC targets, and Iranian media reported a family of three killed on Qeshm Island. Iran struck Jordan and Kuwait on July 30, killing one person in Kuwait. By July 31 the IRGC was claiming tanker strikes in Hormuz and Washington was preparing what CBS sources called one of the harshest bombing campaigns of the war, aimed at Iranian energy infrastructure. Then, late on August 1, Trump called it off — the “biggest attack since World War II,” in his words — saying “the perimeters of a deal has been agreed to.” On August 3 he said negotiations begin. This is the fourth escalate-then-pause cycle since June, and each previous one proved fragile.

The claimed deal is not corroborated by the other party, and that is the central caveat of this briefing. Trump described terms including the “Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT, and an end to Iran’s nuclear threat,” and said Iran and other regional states had asked him to hold off; Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly urged restraint. Iran’s semi-official Mehr agency answered on August 2 that this was “simply a new lie,” citing unnamed military officials, and the defense minister said Iran was “neither surprised nor passive.” Tehran continues to deny any direct talks with Washington. The “biggest attack since World War II” characterization is Trump’s own and unverifiable. What is verifiable is that the strike did not happen, that no American munitions have fallen on Iran since July 29, and that US forces remain, in the administration’s phrase, “locked and loaded.” Treat the framework as an announcement rather than an agreement until a text or a sustained change in Hormuz traffic confirms it.

Oil traded the diplomacy and ignored the fighting, which is the most useful signal on the board. Brent settled $90.74 on July 29 — up 7.9 percent, its biggest daily jump in more than two weeks — then eased to $89.03 on the Saudi naval-coalition proposal, closed July at $90.12 for a monthly gain of roughly 20 percent, and slumped to about $83 on August 3 on Trump’s words alone. WTI ran the same course to about $79. That is an eight percent round trip driven by an announcement, not a barrel: it measures how much of the current price is war premium rather than physical shortage. Equities moved with it, falling 1.52 percent on July 29 in the worst Dow session since April 2025 — the Fed held rates amid oil-driven inflation fears, the 30-year Treasury yield touched a two-decade high, and chips pushed the Nasdaq 100 into technical correction — before recovering to roughly 7,537 on August 3. July still closed red, the S&P 500’s first losing July since 2014. The human ledger did not move: every toll counter on this dashboard is held, several of them now weeks stale, and the one active dispute is a Pentagon bookkeeping split that put US wounded at 624 or 642 depending on which category you count.

Thirteen Nights, Then Nothing: How the Pause Began

The campaign ended the way it ran — on a schedule. CENTCOM completed its thirteenth consecutive night at 9 p.m. Eastern on July 23, describing the target set as military command centers, drone storage facilities, communication networks, coastal surveillance sites and maritime capabilities, and declining to give a target count. Iranian state media reported four killed and five wounded near Ahvaz that night, and two killed and eleven wounded at the Shalamcheh border crossing the night before; both counts are Iranian-sourced and independently unconfirmed, though ABC News obtained video of the Shalamcheh impact. Then on July 24 there was no announcement, and none on the 25th, 26th or 27th. A Defense Department source told CNN operations were “on a hold.”

The decision appears to have been made under constraint rather than from strength. Iran had rejected a U.S. ceasefire proposal delivered through the Iraqi prime minister on July 23. Vice President Vance and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Caine reportedly cautioned the president against further escalation, citing the state of the American munitions stockpile — the same argument CSIS analysts have made publicly about the sustainability of a $500 million-a-day bombing tempo. Trump has framed the pause as space for diplomacy while warning that strikes will resume “with greater force” if talks fail. Whether this is a deliberate off-ramp or a reload is the single most important open question on the board.

What did not pause is the blockade. The naval quarantine of Iranian ports ran straight through the quiet nights: CENTCOM counted nine vessels redirected and one disabled as of July 24, rising to twelve redirected, two disabled and two boarded by July 26. Iran is running a mirror-image regime of its own, with state TV reporting that the IRGC navy stopped six vessels on July 27 for attempting routes Tehran had not approved. Hormuz traffic remains crushed at roughly eight transits a day against more than a hundred before the war. The guns are quiet; the chokehold is not.

Counting the Dead Twice: The Pentagon’s Accounting Problem

The most troubling development of the window was not kinetic. On July 23 the Pentagon’s Defense Casualty Analysis System showed 14 Americans killed and 420 wounded in the Iran war. Three days later, after congressional and media scrutiny, the same system showed 18 killed and 624 wounded. Nothing had happened in between; the difference was bookkeeping. The July 26 update restored four soldiers who had been dropped — 1st Lt. Tyler James Feehan, 25, Pvt. Isabella Gonzales, 19, and Sgt. Angel S. Rampersad, 28, all killed July 17 at Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, and Staff Sgt. Michael Emmanuel Swinton, killed July 19 in a drone detonation at Erbil — and added more than 140 wounded.

It also created something new: an “Overseas Operations (since Jul 7)” category held separate from Operation Epic Fury, with 207 of the wounded logged under it. Only by summing the two does one arrive at 18 killed and 624 wounded since February 28, against a prior wounded peak of 482. Lawmakers and reporters criticized the split as obscuring the war’s true toll, and the criticism is hard to dismiss when the reorganization’s first visible effect was a lower headline number. This dashboard treats the July 26 restored figures as authoritative while surfacing the dispute rather than presenting a single unqualified count.

The episode is a warning about every casualty number on this board. Iran’s tolls are no more settled: HRANA documents 3,636 dead (1,701 civilian, 1,221 military, 714 unclassified), the Iran Foundation of Martyrs counts 3,468, and U.S.-Israeli estimates run past 6,000 — figures that must be displayed by reporting body and never averaged into a false consensus. Iran’s Health Ministry raised its period tally for U.S. strikes since June 27 to 59 killed and 666 injured on July 27, up four deaths from July 24 despite no strikes in between, which is what deaths of the previously wounded look like in a lagging count. Where a number cannot be sourced — displacement, cumulative flight cancellations, Iranian child casualties — this dashboard holds the prior value and says so rather than inventing movement.

The Dual-Chokepoint Squeeze and an $88 Barrel

Saudi Arabia spent this war building a way around Hormuz, and on July 25 the Houthis showed they can reach it. Ballistic missiles, cruise missiles and drones struck Aramco facilities at Jizan, where a large smoke plume and a NASA FIRMS thermal anomaly recorded around 01:17 UTC confirm a fire at the 400,000-barrel-a-day refinery, and at Yanbu, where two ballistic missiles were intercepted by a Patriot battery operated by the Greek military. Riyadh and Aramco have released no damage or casualty assessment. Spokesman Yahya Saree framed it as retaliation for Saudi-led coalition strikes on Hodeidah and Kamaran Island the previous day, and it is the first Houthi strike on Saudi oil since 2022. The following day the Houthis claimed two further “qualitative operations” on the Red Sea coast, and Saudi air defenses downed Iranian-backed Iraqi-militia drones aimed at petroleum facilities.

Yanbu is the point. With Hormuz throttled to roughly eight transits a day, Yanbu became the kingdom’s export lifeline — about 92 percent of Saudi seaborne crude in June (Kpler/AFP), with loadings hitting a record 4.7 million barrels a day around July 13 (Signal Ocean). A chokepoint bypass only works if the bypass is safe. Red Sea war-risk premiums had already risen from about 0.3 to 0.75 percent of vessel value after the Encelia attack, five tankers had rerouted by July 22, and Asian buyers are weighing Suez and Cape of Good Hope diversions. The question for the next cycle is whether the Houthis attempt a third node — Ras Tanura, or Aramco processing — and whether Bab el-Mandeb effectively closes.

Against that, the barrel fell anyway, which tells you what the market is actually pricing. Brent touched $101.01 intraday on July 23, settled $97.24 on July 24 as the strikes stopped, and collapsed to $88.36 on July 27 — down 8.7 percent, the biggest single-session drop since April 8, having fallen as much as 7.4 percent below $90 intraday before paring. WTI settled $82.61, down 7.5 percent, helped further by resumed Caspian Pipeline Consortium loadings. CNN put Brent lower still, at $85.87 and down 11.3 percent; this dashboard uses CNBC’s $88.36 because it explicitly cites the September contract settle, and flags both. Oil is now the cleanest real-time barometer of this conflict: the $101-to-$88 swing tracked the pause almost tick for tick, and a re-break above $95 would signal the pause is failing before any strike announcement arrives. Equities barely moved — the S&P 500 closed July 27 at 7,413.18, up 0.02 percent, its relief rally spent by the bell — while the pump, lagging crude by two to three weeks, kept climbing to $4.110.

Talking Without Talking: Mediation, Hormuz, and a Washington Meeting

Three days of quiet produced the first optimistic read in weeks — and immediately a contradictory one. Regional officials told the Associated Press on July 27 that mediators led by Qatar and Pakistan had made “significant” progress toward restoring the collapsed interim ceasefire. A day earlier, an Al Jazeera source close to the mediation had said there was “not much progress yet” amid “fundamental differences” and “deep mistrust.” Both can be true of a process moving in fits; neither is a settled fact, and this dashboard presents the trajectory as evolving rather than resolved. What is not in dispute is the format: Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman Baghaei confirmed there are no direct negotiations with the United States, only messages passed through intermediaries.

The substantive track is the Strait. Iran and Oman held talks on July 24–25 on a mechanism to manage Hormuz transit — the most concrete diplomatic artifact of the window. Tehran’s position is that the collapsed interim deal entitles it to manage the waterway, and it objects specifically to the U.S.-supported Oman-side route. That is a narrow, technical disagreement of the kind that can actually be drafted around, which is more than can be said for the nuclear file. If a Hormuz mechanism is announced, it would be the first genuine de-escalatory instrument since the ceasefire collapsed on July 7.

Everything else remains leverage. Trump said he had “plenty of time” and saw a good chance of a deal, while warning that strikes would resume “with greater force” if talks fail — a posture that reads as deliberate ambiguity rather than commitment. Prime Minister Netanyahu arrived in Washington with Iran atop the agenda, a meeting whose outcome could pull the pause in either direction. In Congress, the $87.6 billion supplemental — $67.1 billion of it for the Pentagon — is still pending with no confirmed markup or vote date, alongside the $95 billion blueprint the House passed 216–214 on July 22 and a Pentagon continuing resolution through December 4. Washington is still budgeting for a long war while its diplomats test whether this one has stopped.
