Situation Summary

For the first time since the July escalation began, the dominant vector is de-escalation. CENTCOM completed its thirteenth consecutive night of strikes at 9 p.m. Eastern on July 23 — and then, on July 24, announced nothing. It was the first night off in two weeks. A Defense Department source told CNN operations were “on a hold.” Iran read the signal and answered it: its IRGC fired a twenty-seventh wave of Operation Nasr-2 at Gulf bases on July 24, then stopped. A mutual pause has now held for three straight days, July 24 through 27, with a senior Iranian official telling Reuters that Tehran will suspend attacks as long as the American pause holds. Nothing has been signed. The collapsed interim ceasefire has not been restored, Iran insists there are no direct negotiations with Washington, and President Trump has warned that strikes will resume “with greater force” if talks fail. Day 150 is a fragile equilibrium, not a resolution.

The war’s geography widened even as its core cooled. Two new theaters opened inside this window. On July 25 the Houthis struck Saudi Aramco facilities at Jizan, where satellite thermal imagery confirmed a refinery fire, and at Yanbu, where two ballistic missiles were intercepted by a Patriot battery operated by the Greek military — the first Houthi strike on Saudi oil since 2022, announced as retaliation for Saudi-led strikes on Hodeidah a day earlier. The strategic point is the dual-chokepoint squeeze: with Hormuz throttled to roughly eight transits a day against more than a hundred before the war, Yanbu had become Saudi Arabia’s export lifeline, handling about 92 percent of its seaborne crude in June and hitting record loadings of 4.7 million barrels a day around July 13. The Houthis have now demonstrated they can reach it. Separately, and on the same day, Iran accused Ukraine of striking an Iranian commercial vessel in the Caspian Sea, killing one sailor — the clearest overlap yet between the Middle East and Ukraine wars, and a theater in which Tehran has promised a response.

Oil priced the pause almost tick for tick, and it is now the cleanest real-time barometer of this conflict. Brent touched $101.01 intraday on July 23 — its first break above $100 since late May — settled $97.24 on July 24 as the strikes stopped, and then collapsed to $88.36 on July 27, a drop of 8.7 percent and the largest single session decline since April 8. WTI followed to $82.61. The war premium built over thirteen nights of bombing was surrendered in two sessions. Equities were unmoved by comparison: the S&P 500 closed July 27 at 7,413.18, up 0.02 percent, an opening relief rally that faded by the bell, with the Dow up half a percent and the Nasdaq down as chips dragged. The pump has not caught up — AAA’s national average rose to $4.110, up $1.12 and 37 percent from the pre-war baseline, because retail lags crude by two to three weeks. Meanwhile the Pentagon revised the human ledger: after briefly showing a lower toll, its casualty system was restored on July 26 to 18 killed and 624 wounded, under a new split-category accounting that lawmakers and media criticized as obscuring the war’s true cost. Both carriers remain on station, with the Lincoln now past 207 consecutive days at sea, a U.S. Navy record.

Thirteen Nights, Then Nothing: How the Pause Began

The campaign ended the way it ran — on a schedule. CENTCOM completed its thirteenth consecutive night at 9 p.m. Eastern on July 23, describing the target set as military command centers, drone storage facilities, communication networks, coastal surveillance sites and maritime capabilities, and declining to give a target count. Iranian state media reported four killed and five wounded near Ahvaz that night, and two killed and eleven wounded at the Shalamcheh border crossing the night before; both counts are Iranian-sourced and independently unconfirmed, though ABC News obtained video of the Shalamcheh impact. Then on July 24 there was no announcement, and none on the 25th, 26th or 27th. A Defense Department source told CNN operations were “on a hold.”

The decision appears to have been made under constraint rather than from strength. Iran had rejected a U.S. ceasefire proposal delivered through the Iraqi prime minister on July 23. Vice President Vance and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Caine reportedly cautioned the president against further escalation, citing the state of the American munitions stockpile — the same argument CSIS analysts have made publicly about the sustainability of a $500 million-a-day bombing tempo. Trump has framed the pause as space for diplomacy while warning that strikes will resume “with greater force” if talks fail. Whether this is a deliberate off-ramp or a reload is the single most important open question on the board.

What did not pause is the blockade. The naval quarantine of Iranian ports ran straight through the quiet nights: CENTCOM counted nine vessels redirected and one disabled as of July 24, rising to twelve redirected, two disabled and two boarded by July 26. Iran is running a mirror-image regime of its own, with state TV reporting that the IRGC navy stopped six vessels on July 27 for attempting routes Tehran had not approved. Hormuz traffic remains crushed at roughly eight transits a day against more than a hundred before the war. The guns are quiet; the chokehold is not.

First Blood: U.S. Combat Deaths and a Widening Retaliation

The war crossed a threshold on July 17. An Iranian ballistic-missile attack — at least two missiles — struck Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan, roughly eighty kilometers from Amman and a launch point for U.S. strikes on Iran, killing three American soldiers: 1st Lt. Tyler James Feehan, 25, of Hawaii; Pvt. Isabella Gonzales, 19, of Texas; and Sgt. Angel S. Rampersad, 28, of New York, initially listed missing and later confirmed killed. Four more were medevac’d. A fourth soldier died the next day in northern Iraq during a controlled detonation of unexploded ordnance. These are the first U.S. combat deaths since March, and they move the KIA count from 13 to 17 — though the tally is disputed, with CNN and Britannica citing 16, Time citing 18, and this dashboard using 17 pending Pentagon reconciliation. On July 22 Trump attended the dignified transfer of four fallen at Dover Air Force Base.

Iran’s retaliation concentrated on Jordan, Kuwait, Iraq and Qatar, and increasingly on host-nation civilian infrastructure rather than U.S. bases alone. The IRGC claimed a July 18 strike on Azraq Air Base destroying at least five U.S. aircraft — unverified and disputed by the United States — and, in an unprecedented move, its statement incited Jordanians to attack American troops. In Kuwait, Iranian Shahed drones and missiles set two power-and-desalination plants near Mina Abdullah ablaze on consecutive days, injuring firefighters and a worker; Kuwait relies on desalination for roughly 90 percent of its drinking water. A strike in Erbil, in Iraqi Kurdistan, killed at least nine at a facility of the Iranian-Kurdish opposition group Komala — Iran did not immediately claim it but has targeted Komala before — and in Doha a child was wounded by falling debris even as Qatar continued to mediate.

The pattern is the story. Where earlier phases saw Iran aim principally at U.S. military installations, this window saw it hit the water and power that Gulf civilians depend on — a shift with humanitarian and legal weight that the region’s governments will not treat lightly. Notably absent from the window was any Iranian fire on Israel, which stayed quiet throughout — a restraint worth watching as a potential signal, or merely a pause.

Two Chokepoints: A Red Sea Front and Oil Toward $100

The single most important structural change of the window is geographic: the war now spans two chokepoints. On July 20 Yemen’s Houthis declared a maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia via Bab el-Mandeb, and on July 22 they enforced it, striking the Saudi tankers Encelia — a bow fire confirmed by the Saudi Press Agency — and Layla, whose hit remains unconfirmed, and claiming they forced roughly ten ships to retreat. Bab el-Mandeb is the more dangerous of the two straits to lose: about fourteen miles wide at its narrowest, roughly forty percent narrower than Hormuz, it carries some 6.2 million barrels a day of oil (Kpler). With Hormuz already contested and the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports in force, a live Houthi blockade puts a second major artery at risk — and by July 22 five tankers had already changed course.

That two-chokepoint premium is what pushed crude through a psychological line. Brent, which opened the window in the low-$80s, climbed steadily — $89.22 on July 20, $91.01 on July 21, then a $94.07 settle on July 22, its highest close in over a month — before spiking intraday July 23 to roughly $100.05–$100.08, up 6.4 percent and the first move above $100 since late May; WTI reached about $91.24, its highest since June 11. Settlements for July 23 were still pending at the time of writing. The pump followed: AAA’s national gasoline average crossed $4 on July 20 for the first time in over a month ($4.003) and reached $4.019 on July 21, with diesel at $5.11. Because pump prices lag crude by two to three weeks, the July 23 spike has not yet fully arrived at the station.

The cost ledger is being rewritten in real time, though the figures must be read separately. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth put the direct U.S. military cost at $37.5 billion in Senate testimony, replacing a prior $30 billion official figure; the Brown University Watson Institute tracker estimated roughly $71.1 billion in extra consumer gasoline-and-diesel costs since the war began. These do not sum — one is Pentagon outlays, the other household energy burden — and the older Moody’s $132 billion consumer-and-taxpayer estimate, from mid-June, now undercounts. Daily operations, flagged for manual review throughout the standby period, now sit firmly in an active-ops band: CSIS senior adviser Mark Cancian estimates about $500 million a day (“spending far, far less on munitions than the first couple of days”), while CSIS’s own modeling puts it as high as $1 billion a day at sustained tempo. The dashboard carries $500 million as a documented lower bound.

Politics of a Sustained War: A $95B Blueprint, Dover, and Stalled Talks

Washington is now funding a long war. On July 22 the House adopted a Republican-only $95 billion budget blueprint — more than $70 billion of it for the Iran war — by a razor-thin 216–214, together with a Pentagon funding continuing resolution through December 4; both measures now go to the Senate, where their fate is uncertain and where the earlier $87.6 billion June 24 supplemental remains superseded and pending. The same day, Trump stood at Dover Air Force Base for the dignified transfer of four fallen soldiers — the political weight of the war’s first combat deaths since March made visible. A Schiff-led War Powers vote and the Senate’s handling of the blueprint are the next congressional markers to watch.

Diplomacy is stalled rather than formally dead. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said on July 23 that Iran is “not serious” about talks; a mediator-floated ten-day ceasefire — which would have included lifting Hormuz restrictions — did not take. Trump raised the stakes rhetorically, saying the United States would strike Iran’s Pickaxe Mountain nuclear site “probably pretty soon” (Iran warned it could withdraw from the Non-Proliferation Treaty) and, after the Red Sea attacks, that the United States would “hold Iran responsible” for the Houthis. On July 21 Lebanese President Joseph Aoun met Trump, who said Lebanon has a “Hezbollah problem” but that he would talk to the group. The memorandum’s 60-day window runs to roughly August 18, and the General License X oil waiver to August 21 — making the August 18–21 cluster the next hard decision point, with both a snap-back deal and a formal collapse plausible.

The human ledger moved and remains contested. U.S. KIA rose to 17 — four deaths in the window — with sources ranging 16 to 18 pending Pentagon reconciliation; the Pentagon separately said “nearly 100” service members suffered some injury since July 7, mostly minor concussions, 96 percent returned to duty, though this dashboard holds the WIA counter at 543 pending a reconciled figure. Iranian tolls stay fragmented: the Foundation of Martyrs benchmark of 3,468 is held as the attributed headline, while the independent monitor HRANA now cites 3,636 cumulative war deaths and a ten-day report of at least 48 killed, including a one-year-old in Bandar. Lebanon’s 4,321 remains stale and unverified, carried per-source and never merged.
