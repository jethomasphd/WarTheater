Situation Summary

The “effectively over” framing of November 9–11 did not survive the week. Within 48 hours Iran resumed drone fire on Gulf states hosting US forces, and the sixteen days from November 12 to 27 settled into a tit-for-tat rhythm: IRGC drones against US radars and Patriot batteries in Bahrain (November 13, 15, 17, 18 and 22) and Kuwait (November 18), answered by limited US strikes. The pivotal exchange came on November 17, when IRGC drones targeted a US radar at Sheikh Isa Air Base and US fighters hit an IRGC missile launch site on Qeshm the same day; a US official called them “defensive strikes.” On November 26 Kuwait said Iranian drones aimed at a “vital” air-defense site had been intercepted and their debris killed two Italian nationals. Iran “categorically” denied any operation, and Araghchi called the incident a “pre-cooked recipe for war.” No deal and no formal negotiating round took place.

Strike timing is now openly tied to the calendar. Trump said he is “not rushing” and will “see what happens after the new year starts”; he says the war is funded through roughly the end of January. Netanyahu is reportedly pressing for a joint four-to-seven-day campaign, and the IDF says Israel can strike “at any given moment” without US support. In Thanksgiving calls on November 26, Trump said “we would absolutely hit Pickaxe Mountain,” the underground site near Natanz, if strikes resume, and claimed “we sent an extra aircraft carrier there.” The USNI tracker does not show one: George Washington has left the 5th Fleet, leaving Bush and Theodore Roosevelt in theater.

The war’s most active front is now Saudi Arabia’s energy system. The Houthis damaged Riyadh’s tallest financial tower twice (November 19 and 24, 11 injured), hit Ras Tanura (November 25) and struck the Jazan refinery (November 26), killing a Nepali worker. In Hormuz, JMIC counts 17 tankers struck since November 1, and CENTCOM has disabled a sixth blockade runner. The Pentagon’s first official cost update in a month, $48.3 billion as of November 13, puts the dashboard’s $500 million-a-day model roughly $40 billion too high.

The Tit-for-Tat Cycle: Drones on the Gulf, Strikes on Qeshm

The pattern of the last sixteen days is narrow and repeatable. Iranian drones go after US-operated radars and Patriot batteries on Gulf host-state soil; most are intercepted; the US answers with a single limited strike on Qeshm. Al Jazeera counted four Iranian attacks on Bahrain in six days, and Kuwait’s November 18 activation near Ali Al Salem was its first since early September. CENTCOM said no US personnel were hurt, and Washington has framed its response as defensive: one acknowledged strike on Iranian territory, on November 17, and an explicit denial of the Iranian-reported strike near Qeshm city on November 20. Iran’s other claims, two US drones shot down on November 21 and a “hostile drone” brought down at Sarjangal on November 27, are unverified.

The cycle is drifting toward the threshold that would break it. The November 22 strike in Bahrain was the first in this cycle to wound Gulf civilians, and the November 26 Kuwait deaths were the first fatalities. Tehran’s categorical denial of the Kuwait attack, and Araghchi’s offer to cooperate on a probe, suggest it sees the cost of being blamed. The obvious triggers for escalation are a US casualty or a mass-casualty civilian event in the Gulf, and the Kuwait incident came close to the second. Trump’s remark that NATO personnel are in “various parts of Iran” is uncorroborated, as are the chemical and biological weapons allegations from Trump (November 18) and Israeli Defense Minister Katz (November 24), which Iran compared to Colin Powell’s 2003 UN presentation.

The Saudi-Houthi Front: The Bypass Under Fire

The Houthi campaign against Saudi Arabia is now the most kinetic theater of the war, and it is aimed at the system that lets Gulf oil avoid Hormuz. The Houthis damaged the King Abdullah Financial District tower in Riyadh on November 19 and again on November 24, when 11 people were injured; spokesman Yahya Saree describes a three-phase campaign on the district. On November 25 they attacked Ras Tanura. The coalition reported four drones destroyed and acknowledged no hit, but NASA and Planet Labs imagery showed thick black smoke, a Reuters source “confirmed an attack on the refinery,” and Argus reported “two successful blows,” one at the 550,000 b/d refinery and one at the export terminal. Saudi Arabia and Aramco have not confirmed damage. On November 26 the Saudi Energy Ministry said a strike on the Jazan refinery killed a Nepali worker, injured several others and forced a “temporary suspension of some of the refinery’s operational activities.” Drones and missiles were also aimed at Jeddah port and repeatedly at Riyadh; the coalition reports intercepts, and Reuters says most international flights to Riyadh have been cancelled.

The coalition is answering in Yemen, with casualties that sit outside the dashboard’s tallies because they are sourced only to the Houthis: at least 17 killed in a November 18 aerial assault, 19 killed including eight children in a strike on a civilian boat on November 20, repeated strikes on Hodeidah port, and four killed there on November 23. Red Sea war-risk premiums at Yanbu are about 3 percent of hull value, and OPEC+ meets on November 29 without the UAE. In Iraq, government forces began deploying into Kataib Hezbollah’s Jurf al-Sakhar stronghold from November 20; reports that KH fighters were folded into PMF brigades are unverified, and CTP-ISW says the threat to US interests is not reduced.

Hormuz and the Blockade: A Slow-Burn Tanker War

The strait remains closed and contested, and the shipping war has become steady attrition. JMIC’s November 26 advisory counts 17 tankers struck in Hormuz since November 1, four of them in the preceding 96 hours, rates the threat “severe,” and reports 218 commercial vessels stationary east of the strait. Attacks concentrate on outbound traffic 8 to 18 nautical miles off Khasab. The dashboard’s confirmed-attack floor rises from 102 to 113, and seafarer fatalities from 20 to 21 with ADNOC’s confirmation of the Jordanian crewman’s death; the IMO’s broader count is 145 incidents and 25 seafarers killed. The anchorage backlog off Fujairah and Khor Fakkan has grown to about 171 vessels. Iran claims at least ten mine strikes in the window, but no vessel has been named and JMIC says the claims “remained unconfirmed by official authorities”; they look like an information operation layered on projectile attacks. Traffic counts cannot be reconciled: IMF PortWatch shows about 3 transits a day, the FT about 37, and UKMTO traffic 65 percent below pre-war.

The blockade is tightening. CENTCOM disabled M/V Pacific Archer on November 26, its sixth disabling since the summer resumption, and now counts 158 vessels redirected; AP puts the total disabled since the spring at 15 or more. Enforcement now extends to ship-to-ship transfers, and Trump threatened on November 22 to sink blockade runners amid reports, later walked back, of a 14-tanker breakout from Kharg. Tehran’s stated offer is to reopen the strait within 48 hours of the US lifting the blockade and ending the war on all fronts. The economic strain inside Iran is visible: scheduled power cuts of up to two hours every other day began November 22 because power stations lack fuel, and annual inflation is reported near 89 percent, with food above 110 percent.

Markets and the Ledger: Oil Reprices Saudi Risk, the Cost Model Breaks

Oil’s sensitivity has shifted from Hormuz to Saudi infrastructure. Brent slid from $95.82 on November 12 to a three-month low of $89.37 on November 23 as Gulf flows recovered, with Reuters describing supply as “recovering to levels close to those before the war.” It then gained about 8 percent in three sessions on the Ras Tanura and Jazan strikes, settling at $96.57 on November 26. WTI rolled from December to January after the December contract expired at $83.98; the provisional January settle on November 27 is $84.91. Brent’s January contract expires November 30, so the next cycle will show a roll step. Gasoline went the other way: AAA’s national average fell to $3.5866 by November 25, and CNN found a 56-cent drop in the first 24 days of November, the largest on record. Equities are not trading the war. The S&P 500’s seven-day slide into correction on November 20 (6,910.39) was an AI and chip selloff, and the rebound to about 7,298 by the November 27 half-day session followed the chipmakers back up.
