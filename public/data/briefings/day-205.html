Situation Summary

The week that followed the war’s sharpest escalation was its quietest in months — a de-escalatory “fire pause.” No US strike on Iranian territory ashore was reported for roughly twelve consecutive operational periods, and Iran fired no ballistic missile or drone at any host state for ten to fourteen days; the last host-state salvo was the roughly twenty missiles that struck Muwaffaq Salti Air Base in Jordan on the night of September 8–9. The one significant kinetic event of the window came on September 14, when US forces destroyed two IRGC boats in the Strait of Hormuz — boats a US official said the IRGC had used in an attempt to capture a Navy patrol drone. With the guns largely quiet, the war’s center of gravity shifted to law, politics and markets.

The reckonings were legal and political. On September 17 the UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission found “reasonable grounds to believe” that US forces committed war crimes in two February 28 strikes — on the Shajareh Tayyebeh primary school in Minab, where it documented at least 157 killed including 120 children, and on a sports center in Lamerd, where 22 civilians died — and that Iranian authorities committed crimes against humanity in suppressing the winter protests. The White House rejected the report the next day as “unserious nonsense.” Two days earlier the House had voted 220–204 to limit the President’s authority to strike Iran, its third war-powers resolution of the conflict, and Rep. Thomas Massie filed articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

Markets took their cue from the Federal Reserve, not the war. On September 16 the Fed raised rates 25 basis points to 3.75–4 percent — its first hike since July 2023 — with Chair Kevin Warsh citing inflation partly tied to Middle East energy costs. Oil retreated from its mid-week spike as Saudi pipeline fears eased and Riyadh restored bypass capacity, Brent falling from a $108.75 settle on September 15 to $103.87 on September 18; the S&P 500 ended the week at 7,650.50 after a post-Fed relief rally, and AAA gasoline reached $4.4761 by September 20. And after more than eight months on station, the USS Abraham Lincoln carrier strike group left the Middle East, pulling into Guam on September 19 en route to San Diego.

A War on Logistics, Not People

The defining fact of the week is a negative one: the most intense stretch of fighting in more than a month killed almost no one. The US destroyed five Iranian tankers on September 8, but CENTCOM says the crews were directed to abandon ship first, and no fatalities were reported. The IRGC’s ballistic salvo on Al-Azraq the next night damaged an A-10 and about eight F-15Es and killed no US personnel. The Saudi pipeline strike injured an undisclosed number but was aimed at pumping stations, not people. Across September 7–13 the tracked national casualty lines — Iranian, American, Lebanese, Israeli — did not move.

The single exception arrived on the last day, when an unidentified projectile killed the seafarer Jamshid Rajabi and wounded four aboard an Iranian merchant vessel near Hengam Island — the first tracked death in a week, logged on the Iranian civilian line pending confirmation of who fired. The pattern is not an accident of a quiet week; it is the shape of the war now. Washington’s “tanker for tanker” doctrine and Iran’s answer are both aimed at machines, infrastructure and revenue rather than populations. Expect the casualty datasets to stay flat while the energy and economic indicators do the moving.

Chokepoint Contagion: Three Crude Arteries Degraded at Once

The structural story of the week is that all three of the region’s primary crude arteries are now degraded at the same time — a first. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed since March, with flows that independent trackers now put below two million barrels a day against a pre-war twenty-plus. On September 10–11 drones launched from Maysan province in Iraq struck pumping stations on Saudi Arabia’s East-West (Petroline) pipeline, the kingdom’s principal Hormuz bypass to the Red Sea, and Riyadh shut the line — a roughly 700,000 bpd loss. And by September 11 the Houthis held Mokha, Dhubab and Mayun Island, giving them physical control of Bab el-Mandeb, through which about 4.2 million barrels a day once passed.

That simultaneity is the engine behind Brent’s move above $107 and the first-ever $6 diesel print. With Hormuz shut, the Saudi bypass down and Bab el-Mandeb contested, the market has run out of easy alternatives, and the crude complex repriced accordingly before easing on Hormuz-diplomacy headlines. The fallbacks to watch next are the Suez/SUMED corridor and the Egyptian land route; the potential circuit-breaker is the Salalah transit understanding. Until one of those opens, the energy shock is the war’s dominant transmission mechanism into the global economy.

Diplomacy and Escalation on the Same Clock

Diplomacy and escalation are no longer sequential; they are simultaneous. In the same window in which Iran’s parliament speaker Ghalibaf declared “the era of proportionate responses is over” and Vice President Vance reiterated that there would be no talks until Iran stops attacking Hormuz shipping, the six GCC states and Iran agreed to send their foreign ministers to Salalah, Oman on September 14 — the first such gathering since the war began — to weigh a temporary transit arrangement, and President Trump offered a softer line, saying talks were possible and the war might end after the midterm election.

Two near-term catalysts will decide which track wins. The first is the Salalah readout: a workable transit arrangement, under which inbound vessels use Iranian waters and outbound ships transit mostly Omani waters, would likely cap oil, while a failure would re-accelerate it. The second is the next turn of the US Treasury’s sanctions campaign. The market’s calm — the S&P snapped a four-day slide on an in-line CPI, with the VIX under 16 — assumes the diplomatic track stays alive. A collapse at Salalah is the fastest way to test that assumption.

The harder problem is attribution.
