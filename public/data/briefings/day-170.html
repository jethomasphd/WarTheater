The Pause Holds a Second Week as the War Splits in Two

For the second week running, no American munitions fell on Iranian soil. The stand-down that began around August 1 — when Trump called off a planned strike on Iranian energy infrastructure — has now held toward a third week, the longest quiet of the renewed war. What makes it more than a lull is the target set that was stood down: an attack on Iran’s refineries and export terminals is the line both sides treat as separating a war over shipping from a war over the Iranian economy. Leaving that package on the shelf for two weeks is a qualitatively different signal than the tactical pauses that preceded it.

But the war did not go quiet; it split. The US–Iran axis has become a maritime-and-economic standoff — the blockade tightening, US vessel-escort capacity expanding, sanctions threats multiplying, and Iranian forces answering at sea rather than at the source. On August 13 Iranian drones struck two ADNOC tankers in the Strait of Hormuz, the latest of roughly 15 to 17 ADNOC vessels hit since February; no one was hurt, but the message was that Tehran can keep the corridor bleeding while the strikes on its own soil pause.

The killing that did happen in-window happened elsewhere. On August 15 Israeli air strikes killed at least eleven people in south Lebanon, the deadliest day on that front since the June framework, after Hezbollah drone attacks wounded three Israeli soldiers. The war has effectively bifurcated: an Iran-centric standoff now fought with warships, sanctions and tankers, and a separate Israel–Hezbollah front still fought with air strikes and drones. The danger for anyone counting this war is that the dashboard’s Iran-centric toll lines will keep going stale precisely as the violence relocates to fronts they were not built to track.

Talks Stall: ‘US Territory’ Meets ‘Cannot Be Seized With a Tweet’

The diplomacy stalled and turned rhetorical. With the two governments still passing messages only through intermediaries, Trump declared he would make the Strait of Hormuz American “territory” once the war ended — a sovereignty claim over an international waterway that Iran’s foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, dismissed with the line of the week: the strait “cannot be seized with a tweet.” Araghchi went further, saying Tehran had not even decided whether to resume talks, and blaming Washington for the return of violence to Hormuz.

Iran spent the window hardening, not softening. Its opening demand — unfreeze Iranian funds and lift the blockade before any reopening — was repeated by figure after figure, from Rezaei to the foreign-ministry spokesman Baghaei to Araghchi himself, and Iran and Oman never closed the route agreement that had looked near days earlier. Tehran is treating the strait as its last real piece of leverage and pricing it at a maximum.

Washington answered with escalation-by-other-means. Treasury Secretary Bessent warned on Newsmax of unprecedented measures aimed at the “economic isolation” of Iran, promising further announcements in the coming week; Defense Secretary Hegseth said US forces could hold an indefinite blockade of Iranian ports. Mediation widened — European players were drawn in and Jared Kushner toured the region — but the through-line was pressure, not compromise. The configuration is a negotiation in which one side raises its demands as the other reaches for sanctions: it can resolve quickly if a mediator bridges it, or grind on indefinitely if neither moves first.

A Carrier Leaves Asia: The Structural Cost of an Open-Ended War

The week’s most consequential development was not a strike or a settle but a sailing. To sustain the Iran deployment, the Navy ordered the forward-deployed USS George Washington out of the western Pacific to relieve the USS Abraham Lincoln, leaving Asia without an American carrier on station for the first time in this cycle — a gap analysts flagged as significant given rising Chinese assertiveness, with the Navy suggesting it might backfill within a couple of months.

The ship being relieved is itself a story. The Lincoln has now been at sea a record 240-plus days, extended well past its original May return date to hold the line off Iran. AP reported growing mental-health and supply strain aboard and congressional scrutiny, with Democratic lawmakers seeking investigations; Trump said the record time at sea was “not nearly long enough.” Force-structure strain — carriers held past their limits, a theater stripped to reinforce another — is now a cost of this war in its own right, and a metric worth tracking.

A second gap is in the count itself. The war’s newest casualties are merchant crews: across the ADNOC vessels struck in Hormuz, ADNOC has reported roughly one seafarer killed and twenty injured, none of whom fit this dashboard’s five national-toll lines. A conflict increasingly fought over and against shipping, and spilling into Lebanon, is being measured by a ledger built for strikes on land.
