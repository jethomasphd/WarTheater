The Ceasefire Is Now a Euphemism for Sustained Maritime Conflict — and the Bush Is Now an Active Strike Platform

Day 70 marks the third consecutive day with kinetic exchanges in or near the Strait of Hormuz: the M/T Hasna disablement May 6, the May 7 Hormuz transit-under-fire and reciprocal Iranian strikes against U.S. naval vessels east of the strait, and now the F/A-18E disablement of M/T Sea Star III + M/T Sevda May 8. Yet both President Trump and Iran’s Foreign Ministry continue to characterize the ceasefire as intact. Trump on May 8: “No, no, the ceasefire is going. It’s in effect… They trifled with us today. We blew them away.” The analytical insight that crystallized this week: both sides are tacitly accepting a “no major airstrikes on each other’s territory” floor while permitting maritime, blockade-enforcement, and proxy violence to continue. The ceasefire is not a binary — it is a calibrated equilibrium with a violence floor visibly running at 2–3 kinetic engagements per week. The dashboard’s binary “ceasefire holds” indicator should be read alongside a kinetic-tempo metric distinct from headline status.

The most operationally significant change embedded in the Day 70 strikes is that the USS George H.W. Bush (CVN-77) is now an active strike platform. Until today, the Bush’s arrival in the Northern Arabian Sea on Apr 23 read as a “double the firepower” reserve posture — deterrent presence rather than active sortie generation. The May 6 Hasna interdiction came from CVN-72 (Lincoln). The May 8 Sea Star III + Sevda strikes came from CVN-77 (Bush) — the first confirmed kinetic action by the Bush airwing in this conflict. Adm. Brad Cooper’s statement framed it as continuity (“U.S. forces in the Middle East remain committed to full enforcement of the blockade”), but the operational shift is real: two-CSG NAS posture is now operationally validated for blockade enforcement and the Bush is a co-active strike asset rather than reserve depth.

The tanker disablement tempo confirms the equilibrium has tightened, not loosened. Day 64 = 0 disablements; Day 67 = Hasna; Day 70 = Sea Star + Sevda. Three vessels disabled in four days. Combined with PGSA toll regime (up to $2M/vessel) and Iran’s effective Strait control, this is no longer a ceasefire in any meaningful sense — it is a violent maritime equilibrium that both sides are managing within a permitted kinetic envelope. Watch for any Iranian retaliatory tanker-targeting (Day 70 IRGC denial of UAE missile attribution hints attribution disputes will intensify) and for whether CENTCOM’s 57 redirected + 4 disabled tally moves into single-day double-digits, which would mark a transition from calibrated enforcement to embargo posture.

Israel Broke the Lebanon Ceasefire in Beirut on the Eve of Iran’s MoU Response

Per an Israeli source to CNN, the operation was coordinated in advance with the United States. Lebanese MoPH cumulative since 2 March is now 2,727 killed / 8,438 wounded — +31/+174 since the May 4 floor. UNHCR’s 380-killed-since-April-17 figure is on a tempo well above 10/day, and the Beirut strike marks the geographic re-expansion the dashboard has flagged for two weeks.

The MoU diplomatic text reportedly bundles a Lebanon ceasefire into the Iran-U.S. framework (per Times of Israel sourcing on the Axios package). The operational reality on Day 69 is the opposite: Iran-track and Lebanon-track are now decoupled even where the diplomatic text links them. Israel’s preemptive Radwan strike timed for the eve of Iran’s response is a deliberate escalation signal — either to demonstrate that an MoU does not constrain Israel’s operational latitude in Lebanon, or to provoke a Hezbollah response that would let Israel argue the MoU’s Lebanon clause is unenforceable. Iran Is Banking the Conquest — PGSA Institutionalizes Hormuz Tolls Even As the War Ostensibly Ends

The most consequential structural event of the cycle is not the MoU framework or the Beirut strike; it is the May 5 launch of the Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA). Iran via Tasnim/IRIB officially established the body with an info@PGSA.ir email, a mandatory Vessel Information Declaration (ownership, crew nationalities, cargo, voyage plan), a transit-permit regime, and tolls reportedly reaching $2M per vessel (UNVERIFIED quantum, but the framework itself is verified). The statement attributed to Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei calls for “using the leverage of closing the strait” as part of “a strong Iran” regional order. By Day 69, the PGSA is operational: Lloyd’s List (May 7) confirms only ~40 vessels crossed the entire strait the week ending May 3 versus ~120/day pre-war. The U.S. Treasury’s OFAC FAQ confirms such payments are unauthorized for U.S. persons; maritime-law experts say the regime violates UNCLOS’s transit-passage principle.

This is a sovereign claim on an international strait extracted under cover of war. Even if the “Islamabad Accord” lands and Iran agrees to a 30-day MoU window, the PGSA bureaucratic infrastructure has been stood up to claim Hormuz governance as a war-derived right. Iran is converting wartime control into a permanent permit-and-toll regime, the way the 2014 Iraq oil-export disruption created lasting legal frameworks for Kurdish independent crude flows. The dashboard has flagged the “ceasefire that ratifies the conquest” pattern; PGSA is its concrete implementation. Watch the MoU’s Hormuz security clause for whether it dismantles PGSA, accommodates it (de facto recognition with Iranian permit data shared with the IMO), or simply does not mention it — the third option is the most likely and the most strategically consequential, because silence is acquiescence.

The blockade, meanwhile, tightened on Day 68. The F/A-18 from CVN-72 disabling the rudder of the M/T Hasna in the Gulf of Oman is a meaningful capability escalation: aerial gun engagement of a 333-meter tanker is standoff blockade enforcement that does not require destroyer presence in the immediate threat envelope. CENTCOM’s 52 cumulative blockade turnarounds is a counter that has been growing in single-digit increments for the past week despite the Project Freedom pause. France redeploying the Charles de Gaulle to the Red Sea (Macron’s May 6 announcement) signals European willingness to backstop Hormuz freedom of navigation independent of U.S.-led posture, but Macron’s parallel call for both Iran and the U.S. to lift their respective blockades signals Paris views the dual-blockade architecture as the structural problem rather than a U.S. position to be supported. The geometry on Day 69 is: U.S. blockade tightening kinetically; Iranian blockade institutionalized legally; European naval presence emerging as a third pole — not pro-U.S., not pro-Iran. The MoU framework is being negotiated against this three-pole structure, not the two-pole structure that prevailed before May 5.
