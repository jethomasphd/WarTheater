Situation Summary

The twelve days from October 31 to November 11 turned on a non-event. Allies had been told to prepare for a short, intense US campaign after the November 3 midterms; Democrats instead won both chambers, and the campaign did not begin. An AP Election Day poll found about 60 percent of voters called the Iran war the “wrong decision,” and on November 4 Vice President Vance wrote that “the reason we lost is the war,” which Trump called “largely true.” What has replaced the campaign is containment in three parts: limited “self-defense” strikes on Iranian air defenses that fire at US aircraft, an expanding economic blockade, and public declarations of victory. The US acknowledged two strikes on Qeshm Island, its first acknowledged strikes on Iranian soil since September 1: an IRGC surface-to-air missile launcher on November 2, after the IRGC fired at a US fighter, and an air-defense site in the Suza area on November 6, which Trump called “minor.” On November 7 CENTCOM stood up Task Force Iron Gate to carry the blockade onto Iran’s land borders and Caspian routes. Trump said on November 9 that the war “militarily, is effectively over,” and Rubio told the G7 on November 10 there is “no need for major combat operations” because the same objective is being reached “by economic means.”

Diplomacy moved but did not close. Iranian negotiators spent three days with Qatari mediators in Doha. Tehran offered to cut its Hormuz reopening timeline from seven days to two if Washington lifts the blockade and ends hostilities on all fronts, delivered what Speaker Ghalibaf called a “firm and rational response” on November 3, and sent a nuclear proposal via Qatar on November 5. Qatar’s prime minister met Vance and then Trump at the White House on November 9 to ask for more time. In public, Tehran’s red lines hardened. Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei said anyone expecting a US policy change after the midterms was “seriously mistaken.” SNSC Secretary Rezaei said Iran “will not remove even a single particle of enriched nuclear material” and that the strait stays shut until Iran’s seven conditions are met. The IRGC said on November 7 that range limits on its missiles had been lifted. Trump, at Arlington on Veterans Day, said he wants “a total victory, not a 93% victory.”

The war’s incidence has shifted from US strikes to shipping and proxies. UKMTO, Ambrey and shipowners confirmed roughly one projectile strike on a merchant ship a day, mostly near Musandam. Two seafarers were killed: an Indian sailor on the dhow Al Jusufi off Oman on November 2, and a crew member on the tanker Velos Sapphire about 50 nautical miles north of Qatar on November 5, the deepest-in-Gulf attack of the window. Houthi missiles and drones targeted Riyadh on five days; intercept debris damaged a Saudi Aramco tower in the King Abdullah Financial District on November 8, and the East-West pipeline was “targeted” in the Medina region on November 9. Markets priced the turn faster than the physical system: Brent fell from the expiring December contract’s $117.64 final settle on October 30 to about $92.76 on November 11 (provisional), roughly 21 percent, and AAA gasoline dropped below $4 on November 10 and reached $3.94 on November 11. Official US tolls were unchanged at 23 dead and 865 wounded (DCAS). Lebanon’s toll rose by one, to 4,390, after what the Times of Israel called the first Israeli airstrike in southern Lebanon in weeks.

Washington After the Midterms: Containment Replaces the Campaign

The midterms changed the politics of the war more than its posture. Democrats won both chambers on November 3, and Senate leader Schumer said “we are going to end the war,” suggesting Congress could use its funding power. Rep. Jason Crow noted that Congress has voted on seven war powers resolutions and passed two, but Democrats lack veto-proof majorities and the new Congress does not convene until January 3. Opinion has moved further than the votes: a Reuters/Ipsos poll published November 10 found 71 percent of adults, including 51 percent of Republicans, want the new Congress to pass a law ending the war, and 26 percent approve of Trump’s handling of it. Pressure on the Pentagon is personal. Rep. Thomas Massie filed eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Hegseth on November 9, three of them over waging war on Iran without authorization; House Republicans blocked the effort the next evening. CBS reported that Trump had asked advisers whether Hegseth should stay. Hegseth answered with a November 10 Pentagon briefing citing more than 13,000 targets struck in under 40 days and more than 80 percent of Iran’s nuclear industrial base destroyed, figures Iran’s Defense Ministry called “deceptive.” He cited 18 Americans killed, a hostile-action framing; the official DCAS count is 23.

The lame-duck window to January 3 is the period to watch. NBC reported that some advisers urged Trump to restart major combat before the new Congress is sworn in. The WSJ described a three-way split: Vance and Witkoff favor diplomacy, Hegseth and Adm. Cooper renewed strikes, and Rubio and Bessent economic pressure. For now the economic camp is winning. Rubio said at the G7 in Paris that Trump “has concluded that for the time being there is no need for major combat operations because we are achieving the same objective by economic means,” while warning that Iran would “pay a terrible price” if it crosses red lines. The tools are multiplying: Task Force Iron Gate for land and Caspian routes, a US request that Iraq close all but two border crossings with Iran, Treasury designations under “Operation Economic Outcast” and against eight Canadian money-service businesses and the exchange MEXC, and a $20 million Rewards for Justice offer for information on Iran’s nuclear program. Israel is pulling the other way. AP and the FT report that Netanyahu has been pressing Trump, by the FT’s account daily, to resume strikes, even as President Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot the mandate to form a government and the new Knesset was sworn in on November 10. Trump also said US aid to Israel of about $4 billion a year will end by year’s end, with arms sales continuing.

The Houthi-Saudi Front and the Wider Region

Saudi Arabia is now the most-attacked state in the theater, and the Houthi campaign is the war’s main active kinetic front. Saudi Joint Forces Command confirmed intercepts over Riyadh on November 2, 7, 8, 10 and 11. The heaviest salvo, on November 8, comprised three ballistic missiles, two cruise missiles and a drone; falling shrapnel caused what the Energy Ministry called “minor damage” to a Saudi Aramco administrative tower in the King Abdullah Financial District, where a Reuters witness saw blown-out windows across several floors. Two ballistic missiles were intercepted near Najran on November 6. The coalition also reported a missile aimed at the Medina region on November 5 and drones toward Mecca on November 3, 6 and 11; the Houthis deny targeting the holy cities, so those entries stay disputed. On November 9 the Energy Ministry said the East-West pipeline had been “targeted” in the Medina region and a limited fire was put out with no disruption; it named no perpetrator. The dashboard counts the Aramco tower and the pipeline, raising oil and gas facilities hit to 57+. Houthi claims of “extensive damage” at Riyadh’s King Khalid airport, hits on Abqaiq and Aramco storage, and strikes on pumping stations near Riyadh remain unverified. Airlines have extended Saudi suspensions, British Airways to January 11 and Cathay Pacific to January 31.

Inside Yemen the ground war is intensifying, and its casualties sit outside the dashboard’s counters. A Houthi ballistic missile hit a government camp in Qatabah, al-Dhale, on November 4, killing at least 11 soldiers and wounding at least 60 (Yemeni media via CTP-ISW). Jabal Irf changed hands twice in two days, and government forces advanced in al-Jawf with Saudi Apache and air support. At least 30 people were killed at the al-Hadara market in Taiz on November 6, with the Houthis blaming Saudi airstrikes and the government blaming a Houthi missile. The Houthi Health Ministry says coalition strikes at al-Shariya near Sanaa killed 33, including 13 children, on November 7; Reuters and AP could not verify the toll. OCHA counts more than 313,000 people displaced since August. The wider region is quieter but not still. A suspected drone hit the LPG unit at the Khor Mor gas field in Iraqi Kurdistan on November 8, suspending production and cutting about 2,800 MW of generation; it was unclaimed, though a Kataib Hezbollah official had hinted at grievances against the UAE, home of operator Dana Gas. No militia attacks on US forces were found in the window. In southern Lebanon, an Israeli drone killed a Risala Scouts paramedic at Qabrikha on November 4, which the Times of Israel called the first Israeli airstrike there in weeks. Air and artillery strikes followed on November 6, 7, 9 and 10, with no Hezbollah attacks reported, while Israel-Lebanon talks remain stalled over Hezbollah’s disarmament.

Hormuz and the Maritime War: Confirmed Strikes, Claimed Mines, a Wider Blockade

The strait remains closed and contested, and the information war over it is now its own battlespace. UKMTO, Ambrey and shipowners confirmed nine strikes on merchant ships between October 30 and November 10. They were the Qatari LNG carrier Al Mayeda off Ras al-Khaimah (October 30, all 25 crew safe); a vessel holed off Lima (November 1); the dhow Al Jusufi, where an Indian seafarer was killed (November 2); the tanker Velos Sapphire about 50 nautical miles north of Qatar, where a crew member was killed (November 5); a tanker leaving the strait (November 6); two outbound tankers off Khasab (November 7); and on November 10 the tanker SEA 1, hit by a drone off Lima, and an ADNOC vessel damaged in UAE waters, which the UAE called a “grave threat.” On November 11 Ambrey reported a merchant ship disabled by a projectile in its engine room about 7 nautical miles northeast of Lima. Most attacks are unclaimed. Against this confirmed floor, Iran claimed some 25 to 30 hulls hit by mines or “halted” in twelve days, with heavy overlap. CENTCOM says the mines Iran laid early in the war have been cleared, and Trump accuses Iran of fabricating incidents. Reuters counts 37 attacks in October and at least 10 in the first ten days of November; the IMO counts 130 incidents and at least 25 seafarers killed since the war began. The dashboard counts only confirmed strikes, raising its attacked-vessel floor to about 102 and seafarer deaths to 20, and treats both as likely low.

The blockade is widening on both sides of the shoreline. CENTCOM’s Blockade 2.0 tally reached 147 vessels redirected and five disabled since July after US aircraft disabled the bulk carrier Huda, the first cargo ship disabled in about a month, east of Khor Fakkan on November 7. The same day Task Force Iron Gate was created to target Iran’s land-border and Caspian trade. Trump said it was “in full force and effect”; the IRGC called it “a play on words.” The two sides now claim the same water. Hegseth said on November 10 that “we control the Strait of Hormuz.” Rezaei says it stays shut until Iran’s seven conditions are met, Ghalibaf rules out a “return to pre-war status,” and Iran’s navy told shipowners on November 11 to coordinate with Iranian forces. Oman says it is still working with Iran on a joint mechanism to manage traffic. Transit counts remain in single or low double digits but are not comparable across methods: Kpler counted 14 vessels including four crude tankers around November 8, Windward 6 to 10 a day, and IMF PortWatch 1 to 8 a day against a pre-war average of about 84. About 162 ships were anchored off Fujairah and Khor Fakkan on November 8, and an oil spill closed a Khor Fakkan beach on November 9.

Counting the Cost: Dollars and Lives

The official price tag moved up, and the dashboard’s model has drifted well above it. A CENTCOM estimate obtained by CBS on October 27, and reported by ABC on October 28 as a Pentagon figure of $47.1 billion, puts the war’s cost through mid-October at about $29 billion for munitions, $11.9 billion for operations and $6 billion in equipment losses, excluding damage to bases. That is $3.5 billion more than the prior $43.6 billion figure, which implies roughly $80 to $120 million a day depending on whether that figure dates from September 3 or mid-September. That rate is consistent with the CBO’s $2 to $3 billion a month. The dashboard’s modeled total, accrued at a carried-forward $500 million a day, reaches about $74.8 billion on Day 245. An estimate anchored to the official figure would be about $48.3 billion, so the model now runs about $26.5 billion, or roughly 55 percent, high. It is held this cycle for continuity and flagged for recalibration. The gap could narrow from the other side if strikes resume, since munitions restocking and an added carrier group would push the run-rate up sharply. The wider bill is larger still: the UN estimates $1.4 trillion for regional reconstruction, and President Macron tied France’s cost-of-living protests to the war.

The US toll again moved by administrative update rather than combat. DCAS updates on October 28 and 29 added two Air Force nonhostile deaths, one from illness and one under investigation, raising the official count to 23: 11 hostile and 12 nonhostile, 15 under Epic Fury and 8 under “Overseas Operations” since July 7.
