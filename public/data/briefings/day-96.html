Situation Summary

The defining facts of June 3 were a sharp intensification of the “ceasefire-under-fire” pattern that has become the war’s structural baseline. An Iranian drone-and-missile strike on Kuwait International Airport killed one Indian national and wounded more than sixty, heavily damaging a terminal and diplomatic missions and briefly closing the airport before Kuwait Airways and Jazeera Airways resumed from another terminal; Kuwait expelled two Iranian diplomats. The strike came in retaliation for U.S. “self-defense” strikes on Qeshm Island over June 2–3 — a CENTCOM hit on an Iranian military ground-control station — and a June 3 U.S. Hellfire strike on an Iranian oil tanker near the Strait of Hormuz, whose engine room the IRGC said was damaged. Iran also fired missiles and drones at Bahrain, where the IRGC claimed strikes on the U.S. Fifth Fleet headquarters that CENTCOM flatly denied. Both sides insisted the truce still held even as they traded fire: Vice President Vance said the ceasefire “remains in place but the U.S. reserves the right to launch defensive strikes,” while Defense Secretary Hegseth offered the window’s defining phrase — “for the most part, ceasefire means fire is ceasing.”

Ceasefire Under Fire: An Unsigned MoU and Near-Daily Strikes

“Ceasefire-under-fire” is now the war’s structural baseline. A tentative sixty-day extension framework was reached May 24–28 — mediated through Pakistan, with a separate thirty-to-ninety-day nuclear track — yet the memorandum of understanding remains unsigned, and both sides exchanged strikes around the Strait of Hormuz on June 3 while each insisted the truce still held. Vice President Vance framed the doctrine plainly: the ceasefire “remains in place but the U.S. reserves the right to launch defensive strikes.” Defense Secretary Hegseth supplied the window’s defining line — “for the most part, ceasefire means fire is ceasing” — an almost candid admission that “ceasefire” now describes a managed exchange rather than a halt. No side has formally declared the truce over.

The day’s kinetics were the heaviest in weeks: an Iranian strike on Kuwait International Airport that killed an Indian national and wounded more than sixty, U.S. self-defense strikes on a Qeshm Island ground-control station and an Iranian oil tanker near Hormuz, and intercepted Iranian missile and drone salvos against Kuwait and Bahrain. The diplomatic signals were contradictory. Lebanon is the decoupling wildcard with leverage out of proportion to its formal scope.
