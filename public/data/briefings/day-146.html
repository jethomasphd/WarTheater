The Ceasefire Collapses: Five Waves in Seven Days

The trigger came on the water. On July 7 the IRGC fired on three commercial vessels near the Strait of Hormuz in Omani waters — the Qatari LNG carrier Al Rekayyat took an engine-room hit and caught fire, the Saudi crude tanker Wedyan was damaged, and a third tanker was struck by a drone; there were no casualties. Washington’s response was immediate and categorical: the attack was a “gross violation” of the June 17 memorandum, retaliatory strikes it labeled “punishment” followed the same day, and the license authorizing Iranian oil sales — one of the MOU’s central concessions — was revoked. On July 8, from the NATO summit in Ankara, Trump pronounced the ceasefire “over,” called Iran’s leaders “scum,” and threatened to “take Kharg Island.”

The campaign that followed was broader than anything since the spring. CENTCOM ran five-plus strike waves in seven days against more than 300 Iranian targets: the first overnight wave hit Chabahar — Iran’s only oceanic port, outside Hormuz, struck for the first time since the ceasefire — along with Konarak, Bandar Abbas, Bushehr, Sirik, Qeshm and Iranshahr; roughly 170 targets were struck across July 8–9, another ~140 on July 12, and further waves on July 13–14 extended the map to Jask, Kish, Larak, Abu Musa, Farur, Mahshahr, Omidiyeh and Abadan. An Iranian official confirmed the Bushehr nuclear power plant itself was not damaged. Iranian tolls arrived fragmented and conflicting — the Health Ministry’s 14 killed / 78 injured for July 8–9 was followed by a 17 killed / 115 injured count across six cities, while the Army separately acknowledged 8 military dead — figures this dashboard reports separately and never sums.

July 12 brought a military-history first: three Saronic Corsair one-way attack unmanned surface vessels struck the submarine and ship-maintenance facility at Bandar Abbas Naval Base — “the first time American forces have employed sea drones in combat operations,” in CENTCOM’s words, with a Ghadir-class midget submarine imaged on a gantry at the struck pier. The Corsair — a 24-foot hull making better than 35 knots with a 1,000-pound payload and 1,000-nautical-mile range — had been fielded in theater by Task Force 59 since late March, but a U.S. defense official told USNI the strike was a CENTCOM mission, not 5th Fleet’s CTF-59 and not overseen by the Navy — a command-relationship detail worth tracking as the weapon becomes a recurring asset.

Iran Widens the Target Set: Gulf Energy and a Fourth-Country Death

Iran’s retaliation followed the tit-for-tat template of earlier phases — but with a wider geographic footprint than at any point in the war. After the July 8 U.S. strikes, successive missile and drone waves targeted the 5th Fleet headquarters area and Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain (sirens sounded three times across July 8, 12 and 13, with downed drones damaging Bahraini homes), Ali Al Salem Air Base and Camp Arifjan in Kuwait, a claimed U.S. command-and-control center and drone hangars in Jordan’s Prince Hassan / Azraq area (Jordan repeatedly intercepted four missiles across July 12–14), and — for the first time — radar sites in Oman’s Musandam and Al Wusta governorates, drawing a formal Omani protest. Tehran claimed strikes on “80-plus/85 U.S. sites,” including two HIMARS launchers destroyed; CENTCOM reported no U.S. casualties, and none of the significant IRGC battle-damage claims has been verified by host governments.

The sharper shift is what else Iran chose to hit: energy infrastructure and third-country shipping. On July 12 an offshore oil drilling platform in Kuwaiti territorial waters was struck — the war’s first attack on Kuwaiti offshore energy assets, injuring a worker — along with three Kuwaiti border posts. On July 13–14, cruise missiles hit two UAE-flagged tankers, al-Bahiya and Mombasa, in Omani waters; an Indian crew member aboard the Mombasa was killed and six Indian and two Ukrainian crew injured. The IRGC boasted it had “struck and disabled” two “rogue supertankers.” The death internationalized the conflict in a new way: India summoned Iran’s deputy ambassador — a fourth-country fatality now sits on the war’s ledger, and in Qatar falling shrapnel injured three people, including a child.

A second front reopened at the war’s southern flank. On July 13, after alleged strikes on Sanaa International Airport aimed at severing an Iranian air bridge — a strike Trump reportedly backed in a call with Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman — Yemen’s Houthis fired missiles at Saudi Arabia’s Abha airport, their first attack on the kingdom since the March 2022 truce, and declared the “de-escalation phase” over. The Houthis also warned airlines off Saudi airspace. For a war that has been fought largely inside the Hormuz basin, a reopened Yemen front puts Bab al-Mandeb, Red Sea shipping and the East–West pipeline — the one Saudi export route that bypasses Hormuz — back into play.

Blockade Redux: The Risk Premium Returns to Oil

On July 13 Trump erased the last of the June 17 memorandum’s concessions: the naval blockade of Iranian ports, lifted June 18, was reinstated effective July 14 at 4 p.m. ET, with the United States declared “THE GUARDIAN OF THE HORMUZ STRAIT” and a demand for 20 percent reimbursement on all cargo transiting the strait. The fee lasted barely a day — on July 14 Trump reversed it, substituting vague “Trade and Investment Deals” from Gulf states and declaring the strait “open to ALL Ship traffic except for Iran.” The blockade itself, the durable half of the announcement, took effect at 20:00 GMT per the Joint Maritime Information Centre. The status of the strait is now formally contested: Iran declares it “closed” and its parliament introduced a Hormuz-management bill; the U.S. and JMIC say it remains open via the expanded southern route, at a “severe” threat level.

Functionally, the water tells its own story. Hormuz crossings collapsed roughly 52 percent week-on-week across July 10–12 as “defensive routing” took hold — just six ships transited on Sunday July 12, against 108 crossings over the July 3–5 weekend — and about 22 vessels crossed in the final twenty-four hours before the blockade deadline (14 entering the Gulf: 11 cargo ships and 3 tankers). Traffic is reverting to Iranian and AIS-dark routes, and war-risk premiums are rising sharply per Lloyd’s List Intelligence. The geopolitical risk premium that had drained out of crude through early July came back in one session: Brent settled up $7.29, or 9.59 percent, at $83.30 on July 13 — its biggest daily percentage gain since May 2020 and highest settlement since June 12 — with WTI up 9.4 percent at $78.14; both traded roughly 2 percent higher intraday July 14 (~$85 / ~$80). Within the window Brent swung from about $71 to about $85, a ~19 percent round trip.

Equities absorbed the shock unevenly. The S&P 500 fell 0.79 percent to 7,515.34 on July 13 after rising intra-week to 7,575.39; the Nasdaq dropped 1.55 percent as SK Hynix — fresh off its July 10 Nasdaq debut — fell 9 percent to lead a chip rout, while energy (XLE, up about 3 percent) was the S&P’s best sector. AAA’s national gasoline average turned upward for the first time in weeks, to $3.872 on July 13 from $3.804 on July 5 — and with pump prices lagging crude by two to three weeks, the July spike has not yet reached the pump. The cost ledger, meanwhile, is stale across the board: Moody’s ~$132 billion consumer-and-taxpayer estimate dates to mid-June and now undercounts; the ~$95M/day ceasefire-standby burn rate is invalid from July 7, with CSIS’s Mark Cancian putting a resumed active campaign at $500 million to $1 billion a day; and no new official cumulative figure has been released — the dashboard flags all cost panels for manual review rather than inventing one.

Politics of a Collapsed Peace: War Powers, a Senator’s Death, a Leaderless Tehran

Washington formalized the war’s resumption even while insisting diplomacy survives. On July 10 Trump notified Congress — in a letter invoking the War Powers Resolution — that U.S. military action against Iran had resumed on July 7, keeping forces in combat for another sixty days, while telling reporters that peace talks with Iran would continue even with the ceasefire “over.” The diplomatic track is technically not dead — envoys Witkoff and Kushner remain nominally in play and Trump says a “deal is possible” — but the post-funeral round flagged for July 11 never materialized as a functioning negotiation, and there is no scheduled round while the strikes continue. Policy volatility itself became a market factor: the 20 percent Hormuz cargo fee was announced July 13 and reversed within about twenty-four hours.

American politics lost its loudest Iran hawk mid-window. Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina died on the night of July 11, at 71, of an aortic dissection caused by arteriosclerotic cardiovascular disease — a natural cause, confirmed hours after his return from Kyiv. Some aggregators juxtaposed his death with earlier Iranian threats against him; no credible source links the two, and this dashboard treats the connection as false. In Tehran, the leadership question sharpened: the six-day state funeral for Ali Khamenei concluded with burial in Mashhad on July 9, and successor Mojtaba Khamenei — reportedly seriously wounded and possibly disfigured in the war’s opening strike — never appeared in public, leaving open the question of who actually commands Iran during a live escalation.

The human ledger moved on multiple axes, all of them contested. Iran’s conflicting official counts (14/78, then 17/115, plus 8 military dead and 3 IRGC members) are carried per-source; state media added a string of individually named deaths — a firefighter at Iranshahr airport, a security guard at a Mahshahr pumping station, a telecom technician on Farur Island. Lebanon’s cumulative toll was reportedly raised to 4,321 through July 8, but only via aggregated secondhand reporting with no primary Health Ministry statement located — the figure is flagged unverified. CENTCOM reported no U.S. killed or wounded in Iran’s Gulf retaliation; the Pentagon KIA count holds at thirteen, with the MH-60S aircrewman lost July 1 still presumed dead but not yet reclassified. The one confirmed new death of the window belongs to neither belligerent: an Indian sailor on a UAE tanker.
