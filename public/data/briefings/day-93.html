Situation Summary

Day 91 was a single Friday in which the war’s endgame and its impasse were declared in the same news cycle — from the same room. President Trump entered the White House Situation Room for what aides called a “final determination” on the tentative sixty-day ceasefire-extension MOU reached by negotiators the previous evening, then posted on Truth Social an explicit demand set — Iran never to have a nuclear weapon; Hormuz immediately open with no tolls; mines removed or detonated; enriched “nuclear dust” unearthed and destroyed with the IAEA; “no money will be exchanged, until further notice” — and concluded with a line that, taken at face value, would end the maritime war: the U.S. naval blockade “will now be lifted” and stranded ships “may start the process of heading home.” Within hours Iran’s Foreign Ministry rejected the framing. Spokesperson statements made clear that “we are focused on ending the war, and there are no negotiations on the nuclear issue,” and Speaker and chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf added the line that defines Tehran’s posture: “we have no trust in guarantees or words — only actions are the measure.”

The agreement, in other words, was simultaneously announced and denied, and as of the Friday close it remains unsigned. The market, however, traded the announcement. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,580.08 (+0.22 percent), its fourth straight record and its ninth straight weekly gain — the longest such streak since 2023 — and the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed above 51,000 for the first time in history (+0.73 percent, around 51,037). Oil fell roughly two percent to five-to-six-week lows: Brent (August front-month) settled $91.82, down 0.95 percent, and the WTI front-month traded near $87.36, down 1.73 percent; Brent finished May down roughly seventeen percent — its worst month since 2020. AAA national gasoline eased to $4.391, AAA’s largest one-day decline since November 22, 2008. The iShares Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA) is now down about twelve percent since early March, the cleanest single proxy for the market’s peace bet.

What is genuinely new is structural, not tactical. Even if the May 27–28 exchange of fire near Bandar Abbas and Kuwait persisted in the background, no fresh major U.S. or Israeli strike on Iranian territory was reported May 29; the day was driven by diplomacy. The cumulative U.S. naval blockade tally had reached 115 commercial vessels redirected by CENTCOM — up from 100 a week earlier — before Trump’s announcement that it would be lifted. With USS Gerald R. Ford home in Norfolk after a 326-day deployment (the longest post-Vietnam U.S. carrier deployment, breaking USS Abraham Lincoln’s 2020 record of 295 days), only USS Abraham Lincoln and USS George H.W. Bush remain forward in U.S. Fifth Fleet, with no replacement strike group ordered — a thinning of forward presence that materially constrains the “at a much higher level” escalation Trump has threatened if the deal collapses. The Lebanon track moved in parallel: Lebanon-Israel direct security talks proceeded in Washington May 29, even as the cumulative Lebanese toll stood at 3,213 (Lebanese Health Ministry, May 26) to 3,269 (CNN, May 27) and an additional nineteen-plus were killed across Tyre, Sidon, Adloun, and the Beirut suburb of Shuwayfat on May 28 alone.

The Trump Demand Set: Lifting the Blockade Without a Signature

The most consequential development of Day 91 is not a signed agreement but a unilateral declaration. Trump entered the Situation Room with the National Security Council, the joint chiefs, and his lead envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, and then posted on Truth Social what is functionally an outline of terms-from-Washington: Iran is never to acquire a nuclear weapon; the Strait of Hormuz must be immediately open with no tolls; all Iranian sea mines must be removed or detonated within a stated window; the enriched “nuclear dust” must be unearthed and destroyed in coordination with the IAEA; and “no money will be exchanged, until further notice.” He then announced that the U.S. naval blockade “will now be lifted” and that stranded ships “may start the process of heading home.” If implemented, that line ends the maritime war and unwinds the dashboard’s primary economic-pressure mechanism.

Iran’s response — arriving within hours and through multiple official channels — was that there is no deal. The Foreign Ministry said Tehran is “focused on ending the war, and there are no negotiations on the nuclear issue.” Speaker Ghalibaf, who is also the chief negotiator, added “we have no trust in guarantees or words — only actions are the measure.” These are not throwaway lines: they specifically reject the structural premise of the Trump demand set, which makes a nuclear-track concession (HEU disposal with IAEA oversight) the price of sanctions relief and asset unfreezing. The carry-forward from the previous day is that ABC has reported any deal would also require Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei’s approval. The dashboard’s editorial posture is therefore unchanged from Day 90: the tentative sixty-day MOU reached by negotiators is a DRAFT, flagged UNSIGNED, and the asymmetry between Washington’s announcement and Tehran’s denial is the load-bearing fact of the day.

The blockade-lift announcement is operationally premature if Tehran has not signed off — ships departing under freedom-of-navigation can re-encounter mines, IRGC fast-boat harassment, or unilateral Iranian tolls, and ADNOC’s CEO publicly said full Hormuz flows will not return before Q1–Q2 2027 even if the war ended immediately. The interpretive read, then, is that Trump has used the Situation Room as a forcing function: he has put the lift on the table to test whether Tehran will accept his terms-from-Washington rather than continue to bargain over the unsigned MOU’s sanctions/uranium clauses. The clearest near-term signals are (a) whether CENTCOM’s blockade tally stops climbing from 115, (b) whether mine-clearance ships (notably the UK Royal Fleet Auxiliary RFA Lyme Bay in Gibraltar) deploy, and (c) whether IRGC tolling rhetoric translates into actual seizures of redirected commercial traffic. None of those signals had cleared by the May 29 close.

Carrier Thinning: Two CSGs Forward, No Replacement Ordered

The structural development underneath the diplomacy is that U.S. forward presence in CENTCOM has thinned to a level that constrains the “at a much higher level” escalation Trump has threatened. With USS Gerald R. Ford home in Norfolk after a 326-day deployment — the longest post-Vietnam U.S. carrier deployment, breaking USS Abraham Lincoln’s 2020 record of 295 days; the all-time post-WWII record remains USS Midway’s 332 days from Vietnam — only USS Abraham Lincoln (CSG-3, in Northern Arabian Sea) and USS George H.W. Bush (CSG-10, co-located) remain forward. Their core escorts are DESRON 21 (USS Spruance DDG-111, USS Frank E. Petersen Jr. DDG-121) for Lincoln and DESRON 22 (USS Donald Cook DDG-75, USS Ross DDG-71) for Bush, supported by independently deployed DDGs and the LCS detachment in Bahrain. No replacement strike group has been publicly ordered to relieve Lincoln or Bush.

The munitions side of the same structural story is sharper. Per Atlantic Council tracking carried forward, the U.S. has expended more than a thousand JASSMs, more than a thousand Tomahawks, around a thousand Patriot interceptors, roughly forty-five percent of the Precision Strike Missile stockpile, and about half of the THAAD interceptor stockpile. The Tomahawk number in particular has moved: the Washington Post initially reported more than 850 TLAMs in the first month of the war, and the Wall Street Journal subsequently updated that figure to more than 1,000 — Stars and Stripes reports, citing CSIS, that the U.S. has fired more Tomahawk missiles during Operation Epic Fury than in any other military campaign in history, ahead of Operation Iraqi Freedom (802) and Desert Storm (288). Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao’s May 22 Senate testimony that the U.S. has paused the fourteen-billion-dollar Taiwan arms sale to preserve Iran-campaign munitions is the cleanest direct evidence that this expenditure is mortgaging Indo-Pacific deterrence.

The two facts taken together — a two-carrier ceiling on forward kinetic presence and a depleted, paused-replenishment magazine — explain why Trump’s announcement of a blockade lift may not be reversible quickly. Re-imposing the blockade after lifting it would require a degree of force-flow and munitions surge that the Pentagon does not currently appear to have in the shelf-ready position the “at a much higher level” rhetoric implies. That asymmetry — a low-cost deal-pursuit posture against a high-cost re-escalation — is the strongest structural reason to read the Day 91 announcement as a genuine bid for an exit rather than purely as leverage. It is also why Tehran’s “only actions are the measure” line is operationally significant: a delay long enough to force Washington into a force-flow decision could itself be a bargaining lever.

Lebanon as the De-Linked Wildcard: Washington Talks Amid Intensified Strikes

The Lebanon track moved in parallel to the Iran deliberations on Day 91 and is the most likely vector for the entire framework’s collapse. The cumulative Lebanese toll is reported divergently by source and timing: the Lebanese Health Ministry stood at 3,213 killed and 9,737 wounded as of May 26 (Wikipedia, The National), while CNN cited 3,269 killed and 9,840 wounded on May 27 — a discrepancy the dashboard carries with both figures and dates rather than synthesizing. May 28 added the war’s grimly recurrent signature in compressed form: at least nineteen killed across Israeli strikes on Tyre (residential building plus a cafe destroyed), Sidon (apartment building hit, five killed including two children), Adloun (a vehicle hit, six killed, one entire family), and the Beirut suburb of Shuwayfat/Choueifat (three killed including two children, with the reported target Ali al-Husni, a missile chief in Hezbollah’s Imam Hossein Division). A Lebanese soldier was also killed near Nabatieh. Fresh mass-evacuation orders were extended south of the Zahrani River, roughly forty kilometers from the border, and thousands fled Tyre under threat of attack.

Direct Lebanon-Israel security talks proceeded in Washington May 29 in the same hours that Trump entered the Situation Room. The two negotiations are designed to run independently — the Iran MOU and the Lebanon-Israel track each have their own envoys, their own venues, and their own scope — and they do not require formal mutual consent. That bifurcation is a feature of the U.S. design and a vulnerability of it. Tehran has been on record, via its Foreign Ministry’s fourteen-clause framework, that ending the war “on all fronts,” Lebanon included, must be part of any signed agreement. The intensified Israeli strikes on May 28–29, the “beyond the yellow line” framing from Netanyahu, and the displacement of more than 1.2 million Lebanese (roughly 22.6 percent of the 5.3-million population per UN OCHA) create exactly the conditions in which Tehran can credibly stall the Iran sign-off on Lebanon-clause grounds.

The interpretive point is that the Lebanon track can independently rupture the Iran deal even if both negotiating tables in Washington and the Situation Room produce drafts. An Israeli expansion north of the Litani during the sign-off window, a major Beirut-area strike beyond Shuwayfat, or a publicly-attributed Hezbollah retaliation against a major Israeli civilian target are each capable of giving Tehran cover to walk. Conversely, a clean Lebanon-Israel agreement out of the May 29 Washington talks — even one short of a full ceasefire — would close the Lebanese exit Tehran has reserved and force the Iran negotiation onto its merits. Until then, the operational read is that Lebanon is the de-linked wildcard, with leverage out of proportion to the size of its territory or the formal structure of the two tracks.
