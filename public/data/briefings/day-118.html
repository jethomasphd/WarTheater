Situation Summary

Day 118 finds the U.S.–Iran direct front quiet — no strikes on Iranian soil since about June 10–11 — but the post-memorandum ceasefire fraying on two seams. In the Strait of Hormuz, the IRGC reasserted permission-only transit and on June 25 struck the Singapore-flagged container ship Ever Lovely, the first ship attack since the strait reopened on June 18. In Lebanon, the fifth Israel–Lebanon negotiating round stalled with no signing, an outcome the Israeli envoy called a “train wreck.” The defining macro story, though, is financial: the oil war-risk premium has collapsed.

Brent settled at $73.74 and WTI at $70.34 on June 24 — both at or near pre-war levels and down roughly forty percent from the wartime peak — dragging AAA’s national gasoline average to $3.928, a sixth straight weekly decline. The slide was propelled by surging Hormuz transits (about sixty-two vessels on June 24), a sixty-day U.S. Treasury oil-sanctions waiver (General License X, valid through August 21), UAE exports near eighty-five percent of pre-war levels, and Brent’s first flip into bearish contango of the war. The market has effectively priced the war as over, even as the ceasefire’s implementation remains contested.

Domestically, support is visibly eroding. The Senate passed a War Powers resolution 50–48 — the first time such a measure cleared both chambers, with four Republicans defecting — and the White House requested an $87.6 billion supplemental ($67.15 billion for the Pentagon) amid bipartisan war fatigue. CSIS now pegs the total military cost of Operation Epic Fury at about $40 billion (a $34–$42 billion range). Carriers Lincoln and Bush remain on station in the Arabian Sea, the Kuwait embassy reopened June 24, and the memorandum’s thirty-day troop-withdrawal clock has not started because no final deal yet exists.

The Implementation Phase: A Sanctions Waiver, Lucerne Talks, and a Contested Nuclear Claim

The week of June 20–25 marked the transition from a kinetic war to a fragile, contested implementation phase under the fourteen-point memorandum signed June 17. The most concrete step was financial: on June 22 the U.S. Treasury’s OFAC issued General License X, a sixty-day waiver authorizing the production, sale, delivery and offloading of Iranian-origin crude through August 21, permitting dollar-denominated payments and even U.S. import — the broadest sanctions relief Washington has extended Tehran since 1979. Treasury Secretary Bessent tied it explicitly to Iran’s pledges on Hormuz free transit and the return of IAEA inspectors. The same day, the first U.S.–Iran implementation talks convened at the Bürgenstock complex over Lake Lucerne, with Vance, Witkoff and Kushner facing Qalibaf and Araghchi, Pakistan and Qatar mediating, a roadmap to a sixty-day final deal, and a new de-confliction cell.

The diplomacy was shadowed by friction. Even as his envoys negotiated, President Trump posted threats on Truth Social to “hit Iran very hard again,” and on June 23 he claimed Iran had “fully and completely agreed to highest level Nuclear inspections… (Infinity!!!).” Tehran flatly denied it: foreign-ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said there were “no new commitments” beyond the memorandum, and the IRGC-linked Tasnim agency disputed the IAEA-access claims. The dispute is unresolved and consequential, because the sequencing of the deal — immediate export waivers and blockade lift, but back-loaded sanctions termination and the at-least-$300-billion reconstruction fund — gives each side room to contest what was actually promised.

On the force-posture question, nothing has moved toward a drawdown. The U.S. Embassy in Kuwait reopened June 24, with Secretary of State Rubio holding a flag-raising during a Gulf tour of the UAE, Kuwait and Bahrain — a re-establishment after the March suspension, not a withdrawal. Per the memorandum’s own text, the U.S. obligation to remove its forces from the proximity of Iran triggers only “within 30 days after the final Deal,” and no final deal yet exists; the sixty-day negotiating clock runs from June 17, so the withdrawal clock has not started. Carriers Lincoln and Bush remain on station in the Arabian Sea (USNI’s June 22 tracker), and Vance said IRGC and U.S. military representatives would be posted in Doha as a direct de-confliction channel.

Markets and Oil: The War-Risk Premium Collapses

The dominant macro signal of the window was the collapse of the oil war-risk premium. Brent fell from $80.57 on June 19 to $73.74 on June 24, and WTI from $77.54 to $70.34 — both at or near pre-war levels and down roughly forty percent from the wartime peak, with WTI’s settle near or below $70 the first since March 2. Four forces drove it: surging Strait of Hormuz transits (about sixty-two vessels on June 24, per Windward AI), the OFAC sixty-day sanctions waiver, UAE exports running near eighty-five percent of pre-war levels, and Brent’s prompt spread flipping into bearish contango for the first time since the war began. Trump, frustrated that pump prices had not fallen as fast as crude, ordered the Justice Department on June 24 to probe alleged “gouging” by oil companies.

Pump prices did follow, with the usual lag. AAA’s national gasoline average eased to $3.928 on June 25, a sixth straight weekly decline and below $4.00 since June 18, down from a wartime peak around $4.55. Equities, by contrast, were choppy and war-agnostic: the S&P 500 fell 1.44 percent to 7,365.46 on June 23 in a global tech and chip selloff (the Nasdaq dropped 2.21 percent and South Korea’s KOSPI 9.99 percent), then steadied at 7,358.22 on June 24 (down 0.10 percent) as the Dow rose 0.35 percent to 51,848.90. Gold slipped below $4,000 for the first time since November 2025, and Alphabet replaced Verizon in the Dow.

The cumulative bill remains large and scope-dependent. CSIS analysts Mark Cancian and Chris Park now estimate the total cost of Operation Epic Fury at “around $40 billion ($34 billion to $42 billion),” spanning deployment and redeployment, munitions, operational tempo, equipment losses, base damage and fuel prices — distinct from the $29 billion official Pentagon operational figure (May 12) and from Moody’s ~$132 billion estimate of the cost to U.S. consumers and taxpayers. Layered on top is the White House’s June 24 request for an $87.6 billion supplemental, of which $67.15 billion is for the Pentagon. The macro shadow is global: the World Bank has cut its 2026 world-growth forecast to 2.5 percent, its lowest since the pandemic shock.

Hormuz: Iran Reasserts Control and Strikes the Ever Lovely

For three days the strait looked like a recovery story. The CBS-tracked count of transits ran about thirty-five to thirty-six ships a day on June 22–23 — the busiest since the war — and climbed to roughly sixty-two vessels on June 24 (Windward AI), with UAE exports near eighty-five percent of pre-war levels, even as more than five hundred vessels were still estimated to be waiting to exit the Gulf against a pre-war baseline of about 120–130 a day. Then Iran reminded everyone who controls the chokepoint. On June 24, Oman and the UN’s IMO announced a fee-free southern corridor; the IRGC rejected it, insisting on Iranian-designated routes and permission-only transit, and broadcast VHF Channel 16 warnings that unauthorized passage was “unacceptable and dangerous.”

On June 25 the warning became a strike. An unknown projectile — likely a drone, per a maritime-security source — hit the Singapore-flagged container ship Ever Lovely on its starboard side about 7.5 nautical miles southeast of Dahit, Oman, damaging the bridge but causing no casualties and no environmental harm; the vessel continued onward. U.S. officials attributed the strike to the IRGC — the first ship attack since the June 17–18 reopening. The chilling effect was immediate: three oil tankers turned back and three diverted, and the UN’s IMO paused its plan to evacuate roughly eleven thousand seafarers still stranded around the Gulf.

The episode fits a clear strategic logic. Iran is not relinquishing the strait; it is converting control into leverage, and is reportedly seeking about $40 billion a year in “security and environmental” fees once the memorandum’s sixty-day free-passage window lapses around mid-August. The naval enforcement architecture on the U.S. side has not gone home either — carriers Lincoln and Bush remain in the Arabian Sea per USNI’s June 22 tracker, with no withdrawal ordered — so the central near-term question is whether the Ever Lovely attack draws a CENTCOM response. If it does, the de-escalation narrative and oil’s slide could reverse sharply.

Two Fault Lines and a Domestic Rebuke

The ceasefire’s second seam is Lebanon, where the fifth Israel–Lebanon negotiating round — held June 23–25 at the State Department over “pilot zones” and a partial IDF withdrawal — stalled with no agreement signed. Israeli Ambassador Yechiel Leiter called it a “train wreck”; Axios reported “more regression than steps forward”; and Reuters said any withdrawal remained merely a proposal “awaiting Israeli approval,” while Defense Minister Israel Katz said the United States had not even asked Israel to leave Lebanon. Early reports of a signing ceremony did not materialize. Israeli strikes continued in the meantime, killing two in Lebanon on June 25. Because the memorandum binds the war to end on “all fronts, including Lebanon” yet Israel and Hezbollah are not signatories, this remains the clause most capable of unraveling the broader deal.

The third pressure point is domestic, and now measurable. On June 23 the Senate passed a War Powers resolution 50–48 — the first time such a measure has cleared both chambers (the House passed it 215–208 on June 3), and, per PBS, the tenth Senate attempt to halt the war. Four Republicans — Cassidy, Collins, Paul and Murkowski — joined Democrats; Fetterman opposed; and the absences of McConnell and McCormick eased its passage. The measure is nonbinding, and Trump dismissed the four Republicans as “losers,” but the vote is a marker of bipartisan war fatigue.

That fatigue collides with the bill coming due. On June 24 the White House, through OMB Director Russ Vought, requested an $87.6 billion supplemental, including $67.15 billion for the Pentagon ($21 billion for munitions, $17.3 billion for operations and $21.1 billion classified), plus Coast Guard, farm-aid, Ebola-response and infrastructure line items — down sharply from Defense Secretary Hegseth’s earlier roughly $200 billion float. The U.S.–Iran direct front stayed quiet through the window, but with the nuclear-inspection dispute unresolved and Trump renewing his strike threats, the escalation risk remains live even as the political appetite for the war visibly narrows.
