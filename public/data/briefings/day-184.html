Operation Economic Outcast: The War Becomes a Sanctions Campaign

The defining act of the week was not a strike but a sanctions launch. On August 24 Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent unveiled “Operation Economic Outcast,” a whole-of-government campaign that OFAC opened with more than sixty designations of entities, individuals and vessels and five sectoral determinations under Executive Order 13902 — reaching digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping. Bessent framed it in maximal terms: “we are launching an economic onslaught against Iran’s financial connections around the globe,” and the era of American enforcement being treated as negotiable was, he said, “over.” It is the fullest expression yet of the substitution that now defines this phase of the war — ordnance replaced by sanctions, CENTCOM by Treasury.

The design of the campaign points at third parties, not Iran directly. Sectoral determinations are the legal machinery of secondary sanctions — tools to punish the banks, traders and shippers in China, the UAE, Turkey and Singapore that keep Iranian crude and cash moving. Treasury has reportedly set undisclosed country deadlines, which means the enforcement phase, not the announcement, is the real test. Whether Beijing in particular absorbs, evades or retaliates will determine whether “Economic Outcast” is a policy with teeth or a press conference.

The early evidence is that the pressure is biting. President Pezeshkian conceded on August 29 that Iranian trade had fallen 25 to 35 percent, and Kpler data show crude export loadings down more than 80 percent year-on-year. But sanctions are a slow instrument, and Iran’s answer — a “Resistance Economy” and a push to phase out the dollar — signals a government preparing to endure rather than capitulate. This has become a war of economic attrition, and its front line runs through third-country compliance.

Hormuz Stays the Tripwire: Two Tanker Strikes and a Revenue-Sharing Deal

The Strait of Hormuz remains the one place this war could still reignite. On August 25 two tankers were struck by “unknown projectiles” near Khasab on Oman’s Musandam peninsula — a Qatari LNG carrier that Reuters identified as the Al Rekayyat and a Saudi-flagged crude tanker — and both were significantly damaged, though fires were extinguished, all crews were safe and there was no pollution (UKMTO Warnings 120-26 and 121-26). Axios, citing two unnamed US officials, reported the IRGC had fired at least two missiles; the official designation, however, remains “unknown projectile,” and attribution is unconfirmed. The market’s reaction was the tell: oil fell about 4 percent, traders reading the strikes as a sign the risk of a wider war had eased rather than grown.

Diplomacy moved in parallel. On August 26 IRGC spokesman Hossein Mohebbi said Iran and Oman had agreed each country’s share of the strait’s waters and transit revenue — a revenue-sharing framework that goes beyond the “interim” joint statement of the day before — and on August 27 Qatar’s prime minister flew to Tehran to lower tensions. Yet the strait did not reopen. Iran tied any reopening to the United States first lifting sanctions and the naval blockade and unfreezing Iranian assets under the June memorandum, conditions Washington has not signaled it will accept.

So the chokepoint stays effectively closed. PortWatch counted just three transits on August 23 against roughly eighty-five a day before the crisis, and traffic remains in the single digits, even as US-led escort operations moved more than fifteen million barrels out on one day and Goldman Sachs estimated Persian Gulf exports had clawed back to 15–16 million barrels a day. The revenue-sharing deal is the diplomatic bright spot of the window, but until sustained transit counts recover it is a framework on paper. Watch UKMTO advisories and any IRGC boarding: that is still where a sanctions war becomes a shooting war again.

The most consequential political signal of the week came from Tehran. On August 29 Mojtaba Khamenei — Supreme Leader since March 8, after his father Ayatollah Ali Khamenei was killed on February 28 — urged Iran to “gradually phase out the US dollar” and to build a “Resistance Economy” centered on domestic production. Coming as Treasury opened its sanctions campaign, the message was unmistakable: Iran intends to endure a prolonged economic siege rather than negotiate from weakness. Pezeshkian’s admission that trade had fallen 25 to 35 percent, with crude loadings down more than 80 percent year-on-year, frames how much pain that resolve is absorbing.

The war’s biggest wildcard emerged an ocean away. On August 28 Trump announced on Truth Social what he called “THE BIGGEST OIL DEAL IN WORLD HISTORY” — “majority US control of more than 65 BILLION BARRELS” of Venezuelan reserves at “no cost to the American Taxpayer.” The Associated Press described a US-government and private joint venture giving Washington roughly 55 percent of output across seventeen fields, following the January operation that captured Nicolas Maduro. Analysts flagged it as aspirational: commercializing Venezuela’s Orinoco heavy crude needs well over $100 billion and years, and current output is under a million barrels a day. If it materializes, though, it would be a medium-term bearish force on oil and a potential vehicle for refilling the US strategic reserve.

The hardware says what the sanctions campaign implies: Washington is settling in for an open-ended economic war, not winding down.
