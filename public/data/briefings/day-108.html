Situation Summary

Day 108 finds the war winding down to a negotiated halt. The violent three-night escalation of June 9–11 — a downed U.S. Army Apache over the Strait of Hormuz, three nights of CENTCOM strikes that Trump said included forty-nine Tomahawk missiles, Iran’s retaliation against U.S. bases in Bahrain, Kuwait and Jordan, and a full Iranian closure of the Strait of Hormuz — gave way, on the evening of June 11, to an abrupt reversal: President Trump cancelled further scheduled strikes and claimed a “great settlement.” The land war stopped there. No U.S. strike has hit Iranian soil since June 11; the four days that followed were dominated not by missiles but by mediation, and by June 14 Trump declared the U.S.–Iran deal “now complete.”

At the G7 summit in Évian-les-Bains, France, on June 15, Trump said the deal was “all signed,” with a senior official confirming that he and Vice President Vance had executed the memorandum of understanding digitally; a formal ceremony is set for June 19 in Switzerland. The core terms are sweeping: a toll-free reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, removal of the U.S. naval blockade, Iranian clearance of mines within thirty days, the release of roughly $24–25 billion in frozen Iranian assets, a sixty-day window for nuclear talks, an Iranian commitment not to pursue a nuclear weapon, and an end to hostilities “on all fronts, including Lebanon.” The terms are also contested: Washington described full nuclear dismantlement while Tehran’s draft spoke of “no new commitments,” and Iran’s deputy foreign minister insisted the strait closure continues until the deal is formally signed.

Markets priced in peace. The S&P 500 closed at a record-territory 7,554.29, the Dow set a record 51,671.03, and Brent and WTI crude fell to three-month lows near $83 and $81 — a roughly ten-percent slide across the window — while gasoline eased to $4.065. The human ledger barely moved on hard numbers: no new U.S. service-member deaths, and no fresh Iranian tabulation for the June 10–11 strikes. The conspicuous exception was Lebanon, where an Israeli strike on Beirut’s Ghobeiri suburb on June 14 killed three and pushed the Lebanese Health Ministry toll to 3,783 — a reminder that the front which detonated the whole crisis remains the likeliest to unravel a deal Iran insists covers it.

From Escalation to Armistice: How the War Wound Down in Five Days

The reversal was as abrupt as the escalation that preceded it. Through June 9–11 the war reached its most violent pitch since the opening weeks: after an Iranian drone downed a U.S. Army Apache over the Strait of Hormuz, CENTCOM ran three nights of self-defense strikes across Iran — Trump said the U.S. fired forty-nine Tomahawk missiles, some landing within forty miles of Tehran, and “dropped $250 million worth of bombs” — hitting air-defense, radar and command sites from Karaj and Isfahan to Bandar Abbas, Sirik and Gorgan. An explosion was reported at the Assaluyeh petrochemical complex, and two water reservoirs in Sirik County were struck, cutting drinking water to some twenty thousand residents in an incident Iran condemned as a war crime.

Then, on the evening of June 11, President Trump cancelled all further scheduled strikes and announced a “great settlement” he said had been approved by every regional party. The land war simply stopped. No U.S. munition has struck Iranian soil since; the only kinetic activity across June 12–15 was the interception of Iranian one-way attack drones near Hormuz, which CENTCOM said never closed the corridor. Iran’s Khatam al-Anbiya command had declared the strait fully closed to all shipping on June 11 and claimed to have struck two transiting vessels — a claim CENTCOM flatly denied — but the threat of a wider naval war receded as the diplomacy accelerated.

By June 14 Trump declared the deal “now complete,” and at the G7 the next day he called it “all signed,” with the memorandum of understanding executed digitally by the president and Vice President Vance ahead of a formal June 19 ceremony in Switzerland. Iran’s public posture was more guarded — spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei had said Tehran “has not reached a final decision,” and deputy foreign minister Kazem Gharibabadi maintained that the Hormuz closure stands until signatures are exchanged — but the trajectory across the five days was unmistakable: from open combat to an armistice awaiting its formal seal.

Markets Re-Couple: CPI Tops 4% as Stocks Tumble

For weeks the market had treated the war as background noise; on June 10 it stopped. The catalyst was a U.S. inflation print that put the conflict’s energy shock at the center of the macro story: May CPI rose 0.5 percent on the month to 4.2 percent year-over-year, the highest since April 2023, with energy up 23.5 percent and gasoline up 40.5 percent — energy alone accounting for more than sixty percent of the monthly increase. Core CPI was a far milder 0.2 percent, underscoring that this was a war-and-energy shock rather than broad-based reflation, but the headline number all but removed near-term Federal Reserve rate cuts from the table and arrived the same day Iran struck three Gulf base-hosts.

The selloff was sharp and unmistakably war-driven. The S&P 500 fell 1.62 percent to 7,266.99, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 953 points (1.87 percent) to 49,918.78, and the Nasdaq Composite lost 1.98 percent to 25,169.50; the VIX surged roughly eleven percent toward 22, with an intraday spike above 23. Unlike June 5’s chip-led rout, the leadership told a geopolitical story: airlines fell hard (Delta down about 2.3 percent), defense and industrials were lower, and oil majors outperformed (Chevron up about 1.2 percent) as crude rebounded. After weeks in which the S&P had wobbled around 7,400 on rates and semiconductors, the war had finally re-coupled to the tape.

Oil itself whipsawed across the window before the rebound. Brent spiked above $97 intraday on June 8 (up roughly five percent) on the Iran-Israel exchange, then slid about three percent to a seven-week low near $91.4 on June 9 as truce hopes briefly took hold — before CENTCOM’s post-close strikes — and finally rebounded to settle at $93.10 (up 1.8 percent) on June 10; WTI traced the same path, bottoming near $88 before settling $90.03 (up 2.07 percent). Lebanon, the Detonator; War-and-Talk Diplomacy

The entire escalation chain traces to a single point of origin: Lebanon. It was Israel’s June 7 strike on Beirut’s Dahieh that prompted Iran’s June 8 missile salvo on Israel, which prompted Israel’s strikes inside Iran, which fed the cycle that produced the downed Apache, the U.S. strikes, and the June 10 salvos on three Gulf base-hosts. Iran has been explicit that its restraint is conditioned on Israel halting operations in Lebanon — a condition Israel has not met. That makes the Lebanon file, not Hormuz, the lead indicator for the next cycle: as long as the strikes on Lebanon continue, the tripwire for renewed Iranian action remains armed.

Diplomacy ran on a parallel, contradictory track. Even as the strikes intensified, reporting indicated the United States and Iran had reached agreement on four points in their talks, with Tehran still refusing to ship enriched uranium out of the country — while President Trump simultaneously declared that “Iran is all talk and no action” and threatened to hit Iran’s “plants and bridges.” The juxtaposition is not incoherence but a deliberate coercive-diplomacy posture: Washington is holding the blockade as its primary lever regardless of the kinetic tempo, applying military pressure and negotiating leverage at once. The Pakistani mediation channel — interior minister Mohsin Naqvi’s June 7 written message to Supreme Leader Khamenei — remains the key variable for whether the talk track survives the war track.

The strategic takeaway from the window is Iran’s demonstrated reach. The Azraq strike showed Tehran can range U.S. forces roughly 1,000 kilometers inland, well beyond the Gulf littoral, adding Jordan to a retaliation map that already included Bahrain and Kuwait. Whether or not a single missile got through — and the evidence says none did — the message to three base-hosting governments was delivered. The conflict has reverted from the “blockade-plus-skirmish equilibrium” of Day 100 to a multi-front exchange that re-prices escalation risk after weeks of complacency. The base case is no longer a frozen conflict drifting toward resolution but a volatile standoff in which the next Israeli strike on Lebanon, or the next “lucky” hit, could pull the United States and Iran back toward open war.
