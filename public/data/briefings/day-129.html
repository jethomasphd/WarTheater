Situation Summary

Day 129 closes a week defined by de-escalation and diplomacy rather than combat. The stand-down the United States and Iran agreed on June 29 held for the entire July 1–6 window — zero new U.S. or Israeli strikes on Iranian territory, and zero new IRGC or proxy strikes on U.S. bases or Gulf states. In place of shooting there was talking: indirect technical talks in Doha (July 1–2), brokered by Qatari and Pakistani mediators, concluded with what Qatar’s foreign-ministry spokesman Majed al-Ansari called “positive progress.” Negotiators agreed a mechanism to use part of the $6 billion in frozen, Qatar-held Iranian funds for goods purchases and a channel to flag violations, then paused for Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s multi-day state funeral (July 4–9); talks are set to resume around July 11.

Financial markets read the calm as a durable turn. Brent crude closed the window at $71.73 and WTI at $68.44 on July 6 — near their lowest since before the war and roughly forty-three percent below the wartime peak above $120 — pressured further by an OPEC+ decision on July 5 to raise August output by 188,000 barrels a day. U.S. equities set records: the S&P 500 finished essentially flat at 7,483.24 on July 2, the Dow reached a record 52,900.07 the same day and topped 53,000 intraday on July 6, and AAA’s national gasoline average slipped to $3.804. The war-risk premium, already gone from crude, stayed gone.

The window’s single new U.S. casualty event was not combat at all. A Navy MH-60S Sea Hawk from USS George H.W. Bush ditched in the Arabian Sea on July 1; three of four aircrew were recovered, and the search for the fourth was suspended on July 5 after more than 102 hours — the aircrewman is presumed lost but not yet classified as killed in action pending next-of-kin notification. Lebanon remained the war’s only live kinetic front, with Israel striking ten Hezbollah sites on July 3. Carriers Lincoln and Bush held station in the Arabian Sea throughout; no surge, no withdrawal, and no new USNI fleet tracker has been published since June 29.

The Stand-Down Holds: Six Days Without a Shot

The defining fact of the July 1–6 window is a negative one: nothing was fired. The stand-down that a senior U.S. official announced on June 29 — “both sides will stand down for now and vessels can move freely” — held for six consecutive days. There were no new U.S. or Israeli strikes on Iranian territory or maritime traffic, and no new IRGC ballistic-missile or drone salvos on U.S. bases or Gulf states. The last U.S. kinetic action on Iran remained the June 26–27 Hormuz-area strikes; the last Iranian action remained the June 28 salvo on Kuwait and Bahrain. GlobalSecurity’s Day 125 report confirmed “no new US offensive action against Iran reported,” and the daily calendar ran clean through Day 128 (July 5).

The quiet was rhetorical only where it was tense at all. Iranian officials issued warnings — a “decisive” or “swift response” to any U.S. “miscalculation” in Hormuz, and “harsh and regret-inducing responses” if Iran were attacked during Ayatollah Khamenei’s funeral — but took no kinetic action to back them. No confirmed Houthi attack on commercial shipping or Gulf targets occurred in the window, and the Houthis made no public statement on the Doha round. For the first time in months, the U.S.–Iran direct front was simply silent.

The one loss the U.S. did suffer was an accident, not an engagement. In the early hours of July 1 a Navy MH-60S Sea Hawk of HSC-5 “Nightdippers,” flying from USS George H.W. Bush, made an emergency water landing in the Arabian Sea; three of four aircrew were pulled from the water in stable condition and one went missing, with no indication of hostile action. After a search covering more than 102 hours and 14,000 square miles — involving air wings from both carriers, five destroyers and two P-8 squadrons — U.S. Naval Forces Central Command suspended the effort at 3:00 p.m. AST on July 5. The sailor is presumed lost but has not yet been classified as killed in action pending next-of-kin notification, a distinction that keeps the confirmed Pentagon KIA count at thirteen for now.

Oil Near Pre-War Lows, Markets at Records

With the shooting stopped, the market’s de-escalation trade extended to new extremes. Brent crude closed the window at $71.73 and WTI at $68.44 on July 6 — both near their lowest since before the war on February 27, and roughly forty-three percent below the wartime peak above $120. The proximate catalyst was supply, not geopolitics: on July 5 seven OPEC+ producers — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman — agreed to “implement a production adjustment of 188 thousand barrels per day” for August, with Saudi Arabia and Russia each adding 62,000 barrels a day. The war-risk premium is not merely gone from crude; the tape is now driven by the mechanics of an oversupplied market.

Equities pushed to records. The S&P 500 finished essentially flat at 7,483.24 on July 2 (up a fraction of a point), the Dow set a record 52,900.07 the same day, and the Nasdaq eased to 25,832.67 as chip stocks rotated out. Markets were closed July 3 for Independence Day; when they reopened on July 6, all three rose — the S&P about 0.8 percent, the Dow above 53,000 intraday, the Nasdaq roughly 1.2 percent — as semiconductors recovered (these July 6 moves are intraday, with this update posted before the close). The ITA defense ETF traded around $247.29 on July 2, up about thirty-eight percent year-to-date and near a 52-week high. AAA’s national gasoline average slipped to $3.804 on July 5, still about eighty-one cents above the $2.99 pre-war baseline; pump relief continues to lag the crude slide by two to three weeks.

The cumulative bill remains large and scope-dependent — and the figures are not additive. Moody’s Analytics still pegs the cost to U.S. consumers and taxpayers at least $132 billion, and on July 1 Mark Zandi reframed that burden as roughly $1,000 per U.S. household “and counting,” calling the estimate “if anything, conservative.” That sits apart from the direct military estimates: about $29 billion in official Pentagon operational cost and $34–$42 billion per CSIS. The White House’s June 24 request for an $87.6 billion supplemental — $67.1 billion of it for the Pentagon — is still pending, with no markup or floor-vote date scheduled as the fighting has paused.

Doha Diplomacy Advances; Hormuz Tolls Still Unresolved

The week’s substantive news was diplomatic. On July 1 the United States and Iran held separate indirect meetings in Doha, brokered by Qatari and Pakistani mediators; VP Vance said the talks were “going well,” and envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Qatari Emir Sheikh Tamim on the negotiations and on Lebanon. By July 2 the technical round had concluded with what Qatar’s foreign-ministry spokesman Majed al-Ansari called “positive progress… building on the outcomes of the Lake Lucerne Summit.” Two concrete mechanics were agreed: a way to use part of the $6 billion in frozen, Qatar-held Iranian funds for goods purchases, and a communications channel to flag violations of the memorandum.

What was not settled is as important as what was. Hormuz tolls, the specifics of releasing frozen assets, nuclear inspections and the Lebanon file all remain open, and the frozen-funds question is a genuine three-way dispute: Iran says a $6 billion goods mechanism was agreed (with Speaker Ghalibaf claiming $12 billion of $24 billion would be released), Qatar says the $6 billion has not been transferred, and U.S. officials say no funds move until Iran fulfills its commitments. On July 4 Iran hardened the tolls question, saying it will “definitely” charge Strait of Hormuz transit fees once the memorandum’s toll-free window lapses — with “special treatment” possible for friendly nations — and pointedly warned the UK and France.

On the water the strait kept normalizing. PortWatch counted about 27 transits a day at its last June 28 reading rising to roughly 35 a day by July 5, still well below the pre-war norm of 84–110; the central traffic-separation lanes remain closed by about 80 naval mines, with two temporary corridors — a widened Omani-coast route and a northern Iranian route — carrying perhaps 80 vessels a day between them, and UKMTO’s threat level held at “substantial.” A five-vessel convoy led by the Liberia-flagged laden crude tanker Hafeet transited on July 5 after about eight vessels U-turned on July 4 amid confusion over routing and tolls; Saudi exports ran near 90 percent of pre-war and the UAE was restored to pre-war levels of roughly 3.9 million barrels a day. Talks are set to resume around July 11, after Khamenei’s funeral.

A Funeral in Tehran, a Live Lebanon Front, and the Human Ledger

The week’s dominant image was a funeral. Iran opened a multi-day state funeral for Ayatollah Ali Khamenei (July 4–9), with a tribute ceremony July 3 drawing delegations from roughly forty countries and the main procession moving through Tehran on July 6 — a roughly ten-kilometer route from Imam Hossein Square to Azadi Square that Tehran’s mayor said could draw as many as twenty million. Conspicuously, the new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, did not appear publicly during the proceedings. It was the funeral timetable, as much as the Doha round, that set the pause in negotiations: talks are slated to resume around July 11, once the mourning period closes. Trump, for his part, said the United States is “going to win one way or the other.”

Lebanon stayed the war’s only live kinetic front. On July 2 a Hezbollah attack near Bint Jbeil severely wounded an IDF reservist of the 679th “Yiftah” Armored Brigade (two other soldiers were lightly wounded); the next day Israel struck ten Hezbollah sites — at Bint Jbeil, Beit Yahoun, Kounine and Baraashit. The Lebanese Health Ministry’s cumulative toll rose to about 4,301 killed and 12,199 wounded by July 4, up from roughly 4,178 at the end of June, with about 1.2 million still displaced. The disarmament-for-ceasefire framework agreed in late June continued to founder on Hezbollah’s rejection.

On the direct U.S.–Iran front the human ledger barely moved, and its one shift was non-combat. The MH-60S aircrewman lost off the Bush is presumed dead but not yet counted as killed in action — a reclassification that could raise the Pentagon KIA count from thirteen to fourteen in a future update. No new Iranian deaths were reported in the window; the standing benchmarks stay divergent and unmerged — 3,468 (Foundation of Martyrs and Iran’s health ministry) against 3,636 (HRANA) and 6,000-plus (U.S./Israeli estimate). Stale by design, the hero counters for children killed, flights cancelled and infrastructure damage carry prior values: no fresh July 1–6 reporting supported a change, and none was fabricated.

What to Watch

  • The June 30 Doha talks: The single most important near-term event. Whether a “Joint Hormuz Committee” produces a durable transit-route arrangement — or whether Tehran’s “not confirmed” posture means the meeting slips or fails — will decide if the stand-down holds. Watch for confirmation from the Iranian side, not just the U.S.
  • Whether the stand-down holds: “Stand down for now” is explicitly conditional. Any new ship attack in the strait, or any U.S. strike in response, would restart the cycle — and this time markets, which shrugged off the first round, might not.
  • The transit-route dispute: The U.S./JMIC’s widened Omani-coast corridor versus Iran’s IRGC-designated coastal route is the trigger for any future flare-up. A negotiated lane would be the clearest sign of genuine de-escalation; continued dueling route declarations signal the opposite.
  • A renewed Iranian closure — the oil tail risk: Markets have decoupled from the shooting, but a serious move to close or heavily toll the strait is the one event that would pull the war-risk premium back into crude. With Brent near post-war lows, the asymmetry of that risk is now large.
  • Third-country civilian exposure: The Qatari death at sea and the Bahrain building strike make Gulf-civilian casualties the politically sensitive metric. Watch whether Qatar, Bahrain or Kuwait attribute responsibility — any formal attribution would sharply raise the diplomatic stakes.
  • The Lebanon framework’s durability: The June 26 Military Coordination Group and U.S. aid package are new, but Hezbollah has rejected the deal as “surrender.” Watch whether the framework yields a real de-escalation on the ground or unravels like the fifth round.
  • The supplemental and the domestic vote count: The $87.6 billion request ($67.1 billion DoD) lands amid four Republican war-powers defections and Cassidy’s “objectives have not been achieved” line. Its reception — and any further defections — is the clearest gauge of eroding political support even as the fighting pauses.
