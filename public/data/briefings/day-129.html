Situation Summary

Day 129 closes a week defined by de-escalation and diplomacy rather than combat. The stand-down the United States and Iran agreed on June 29 held for the entire July 1–6 window — zero new U.S. or Israeli strikes on Iranian territory, and zero new IRGC or proxy strikes on U.S. bases or Gulf states. In place of shooting there was talking: indirect technical talks in Doha (July 1–2), brokered by Qatari and Pakistani mediators, concluded with what Qatar’s foreign-ministry spokesman Majed al-Ansari called “positive progress.” Negotiators agreed a mechanism to use part of the $6 billion in frozen, Qatar-held Iranian funds for goods purchases and a channel to flag violations, then paused for Ayatollah Ali Khamenei’s multi-day state funeral (July 4–9); talks are set to resume around July 11.

Financial markets read the calm as a durable turn. Brent crude closed the window at $71.73 and WTI at $68.44 on July 6 — near their lowest since before the war and roughly forty-three percent below the wartime peak above $120 — pressured further by an OPEC+ decision on July 5 to raise August output by 188,000 barrels a day. U.S. equities set records: the S&P 500 finished essentially flat at 7,483.24 on July 2, the Dow reached a record 52,900.07 the same day and topped 53,000 intraday on July 6, and AAA’s national gasoline average slipped to $3.804. The war-risk premium, already gone from crude, stayed gone.

The window’s single new U.S. casualty event was not combat at all. A Navy MH-60S Sea Hawk from USS George H.W. Bush ditched in the Arabian Sea on July 1; three of four aircrew were recovered, and the search for the fourth was suspended on July 5 after more than 102 hours — the aircrewman is presumed lost but not yet classified as killed in action pending next-of-kin notification. Lebanon remained the war’s only live kinetic front, with Israel striking ten Hezbollah sites on July 3. Carriers Lincoln and Bush held station in the Arabian Sea throughout; no surge, no withdrawal, and no new USNI fleet tracker has been published since June 29.

The Re-Escalation: Four Days of Strikes Over Hormuz Shipping

The sequence began at sea. On June 25 an IRGC drone struck the Singapore-flagged container ship Ever Lovely as it exited the strait along the Omani coast — the same corridor the U.S. and the IMO had just designated fee-free — damaging the bridge but causing no casualties. Trump called it a “foolish violation” of the eleven-day-old memorandum. The next day, June 26, CENTCOM answered with the first U.S. kinetic strike on Iranian soil since the MOU: four targets near Sirik — missile and drone storage and coastal radar — hit by six land-based aircraft. Iranian state media reported infrastructure damage only.

It escalated fast. On June 27 an IRGC drone hit the Panama-flagged tanker Kiku, carrying roughly two million barrels of Qatari crude; CENTCOM responded by striking ten Iranian military targets “in and near the Strait of Hormuz” — the largest single-day U.S. package since the memorandum — while the IRGC reported a telecom tower and two water reservoirs damaged near Sirik. Trump warned that Iran “will no longer exist” if the U.S. were forced to “militarily complete the job.” The same day the U.S. Navy’s Joint Maritime Information Center announced a widened bidirectional route near Oman, directly challenging Iran’s claim to control the chokepoint.

On June 28 the exchange jumped to third countries. The IRGC fired ballistic missiles and drones at Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait — where two ballistic missiles were intercepted — and at U.S. 5th Fleet facilities in Bahrain, where an eight-story residential building in Muharraq had its top floor destroyed, with no deaths. The IRGC claimed eight U.S. sites were struck; a U.S. official said no intended targets were hit. Then, on June 28–29, a senior U.S. official announced that “both sides will stand down for now and vessels can move freely,” adding, “we decided to stop all the kinetic activity.” The fourteen-point memorandum had survived its first real test, but only barely.

Markets Decouple From the Shooting

The most striking feature of the window is what did not happen: despite four days of strikes, oil set fresh post-war lows and equities rallied. Brent settled around $72 on June 26 — its lowest since before the war — then firmed only modestly to about $74 by June 30 on the weekend strikes; WTI touched a four-month low near $69 before recovering to roughly $70.7. For the quarter, Brent logged a roughly thirty-percent drop, its largest quarterly decline since 2020 (the COVID crash), and WTI is on track to fall about nineteen percent for the month and over twenty-four percent for the quarter. The war-risk premium has not just faded; supply normalization now dominates the tape, a structural break from the March–May regime when every escalation spiked crude.

Equities read the re-escalation as contained. After the prior week’s roughly two-percent loss — on June 26 the S&P 500 closed below its fifty-day moving average for the first time since early April — the market snapped back on June 29: the S&P 500 rose 1.16 percent to 7,440.43, the Nasdaq 2.07 percent to 25,820.15, and the Dow 0.59 percent to 52,182.74, its first-ever close above 52,000, with Alphabet joining the index and rising more than four percent. Lower oil and yields were net bullish. AAA’s national gasoline average fell to $3.847, six-plus straight weeks of declines, still about eighty-six cents above the $2.99 pre-war baseline. Defense names (Lockheed, RTX, Northrop, General Dynamics) firmed on the supplemental and a $35.3 billion sole-source THAAD award to Lockheed.

The cumulative bill remains large and scope-dependent — and the figures are not additive. Moody’s Analytics now pegs the cost to U.S. consumers and taxpayers at least $132 billion, factoring military spending plus higher energy, commodity and interest-rate costs. That sits apart from the direct military estimates: about $29 billion in official Pentagon operational cost and $34–$42 billion per CSIS. Layered on top is the White House’s June 24 request for an $87.6 billion supplemental, of which $67.1 billion is the Pentagon share — $21 billion munitions, $17.3 billion operations and $12.1 billion classified. The interceptor math is its own line item: the Payne Institute estimates roughly 198 THAAD interceptors, about forty percent of the U.S. inventory, were fired in the war’s first sixteen days.

Hormuz: The Transit-Route Dispute at the Center of It All

Every strike in the June 25–28 cycle traces back to a single, technical-sounding disagreement: which route ships may use to cross the Strait of Hormuz. The U.S. Navy’s Joint Maritime Information Center widened a bidirectional corridor near the Omani coast, the lane Washington and the IMO favor; Iran insists vessels transit only along IRGC-designated routes near the Iranian coast, with permission, and treats unauthorized passage as fair game. The IRGC’s drone strikes on the Ever Lovely (June 25) and the Kiku (June 27) were enforcement of that claim; the U.S. strikes on Sirik and on targets in and near the strait were the answer. This is not a peripheral skirmish — it is the load-bearing dispute of the entire post-MOU period.

The signs of strain were everywhere on the water. UKMTO raised its threat level from moderate to substantial on June 27; roughly sixty IRGC high-speed craft were observed in the western strait on June 27–28; and four OFAC-sanctioned vessels transited simultaneously on June 28, per Windward, a sign of how contested enforcement has become. Even so, traffic kept moving: Windward’s Maritime Intelligence and Operations Center logged about forty-two transits on June 28 (twenty-eight inbound, fourteen outbound), while warcosts.org cited eighty-nine “U.S.-assisted” transits under a different methodology — a reminder that even the throughput numbers are contested.

The stand-down announced June 28–29 froze the shooting but not the underlying dispute. Trump set Doha talks for June 30, with Witkoff and Kushner attending, framed around the Hormuz question; Iran’s senior negotiator Kazem Gharibabadi said reports of technical talks in Doha were “not confirmed,” even as consultations with Qatar continued. The central near-term question is whether that meeting produces a durable route arrangement or merely a pause. A renewed Iranian closure remains the single biggest risk that would reprice oil — and the only event, on current evidence, capable of pulling the war-risk premium back into crude.

Casualty Asymmetry, a Lebanon Framework, and a Domestic Bill Coming Due

The casualty ledger of the re-escalation is starkly asymmetric. The recent U.S. strikes hit coastal military and infrastructure targets — missile and drone storage, coastal radar, and, per the IRGC, a telecom tower and two water reservoirs — with no reported deaths, and no new verified Iranian casualty figures emerged at all (Iranian reporting cited infrastructure damage only, and GlobalSecurity confirmed no new tolls were published). Iranian retaliation, by contrast, struck Gulf civilian areas: a residential building in Muharraq, Bahrain, and a Qatari national killed at sea by shrapnel — the window’s first new third-country death, which Qatar pointedly declined to attribute to either side. Third-country civilian exposure is now the politically sensitive metric to track. Standing cumulative benchmarks remain divergent and unmerged: 3,468 Iranian dead (Foundation of Martyrs, official) against 3,636 (HRANA) and 6,620-plus (Hengaw).

On June 26, even as the strikes intensified, a parallel diplomatic track opened. Secretary of State Rubio announced a U.S.-brokered Israel–Lebanon trilateral framework — “the beginning of the beginning” — creating a Military Coordination Group for Lebanon, an “immediate” $100 million U.S. humanitarian donation coordinated with the UN, and $30 million to reimburse the Lebanese Armed Forces. Hezbollah rejected the deal as “surrender,” and Israeli strikes continued in the south, but the framework is the first concrete structure on the Lebanon file in weeks — a notable contrast to the “train wreck” fifth round that collapsed only days earlier.

The domestic front is opening as a third pressure point. The $87.6 billion supplemental request — $67.1 billion of it for the Pentagon — lands on top of a War Powers resolution that drew four Republican defections: Senators Lisa Murkowski, Susan Collins, Bill Cassidy and Rand Paul. Cassidy’s line captures the mood: “This was supposed to last four weeks. It’s lasted four months. Our original objectives have not been achieved.” The kinetic activity has paused, but the fiscal and political costs are still climbing — and with no final deal, the memorandum’s thirty-day troop-withdrawal clock has not even started to run.

What to Watch

  • The June 30 Doha talks: The single most important near-term event. Whether a “Joint Hormuz Committee” produces a durable transit-route arrangement — or whether Tehran’s “not confirmed” posture means the meeting slips or fails — will decide if the stand-down holds. Watch for confirmation from the Iranian side, not just the U.S.
  • Whether the stand-down holds: “Stand down for now” is explicitly conditional. Any new ship attack in the strait, or any U.S. strike in response, would restart the cycle — and this time markets, which shrugged off the first round, might not.
  • The transit-route dispute: The U.S./JMIC’s widened Omani-coast corridor versus Iran’s IRGC-designated coastal route is the trigger for any future flare-up. A negotiated lane would be the clearest sign of genuine de-escalation; continued dueling route declarations signal the opposite.
  • A renewed Iranian closure — the oil tail risk: Markets have decoupled from the shooting, but a serious move to close or heavily toll the strait is the one event that would pull the war-risk premium back into crude. With Brent near post-war lows, the asymmetry of that risk is now large.
  • Third-country civilian exposure: The Qatari death at sea and the Bahrain building strike make Gulf-civilian casualties the politically sensitive metric. Watch whether Qatar, Bahrain or Kuwait attribute responsibility — any formal attribution would sharply raise the diplomatic stakes.
  • The Lebanon framework’s durability: The June 26 Military Coordination Group and U.S. aid package are new, but Hezbollah has rejected the deal as “surrender.” Watch whether the framework yields a real de-escalation on the ground or unravels like the fifth round.
  • The supplemental and the domestic vote count: The $87.6 billion request ($67.1 billion DoD) lands amid four Republican war-powers defections and Cassidy’s “objectives have not been achieved” line. Its reception — and any further defections — is the clearest gauge of eroding political support even as the fighting pauses.
