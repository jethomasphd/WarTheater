Situation Summary

The two weeks from September 18 to October 1 turned the war from a contest of strikes into a coercive pause: diplomacy at the front, a military buildup behind it. No US or Israeli strike on Iranian territory was confirmed in the window — the last CENTCOM-announced strikes ashore remain those of September 1 — and GlobalSecurity counted, as of September 27, nineteen consecutive days without an Iranian military death attributable to coalition action and eighteen without direct Iranian fire on a host state. Claims of late-September strikes on Tehran, Semnan and the Darkhovin nuclear site circulated widely but match events from July and were rejected.

The diplomacy ran through the UN General Assembly. US and Iranian delegations met for three hours on September 22; on September 24 Iran handed Washington a written road map; and on September 25 Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days in exchange for frozen funds, an oil-sanctions waiver, an end to the blockade and a regional ceasefire including Lebanon. President Trump rejected the plan as unacceptable on September 26, then sent a formal response through Qatar that Tehran received on September 30. The Wall Street Journal reported he has told aides he expects to resume bombing after the November 3 midterms — and on October 1 US officials confirmed a third carrier strike group, a second Marine expeditionary unit and 9,000 to 10,000 more sailors and Marines are bound for the region by the end of November.

The shooting that did happen came mostly from Yemen. The Houthis fired ballistic missiles and drones at Taif, Yanbu, Riyadh, Khamis Mushait, Jazan and Medina; the Saudi-led coalition reported interceptions, while the Houthis claimed hits that remain unverified. The war’s accounting also shifted: the Pentagon’s official US toll rose to 19 killed and 861 wounded, the latter including eight Marines hurt by an Iranian cruise missile on September 14 that was disclosed only on September 27. Brent swung between $99.25 and $106.60 and finished October 1 at $102.31 on the December contract, up 4.4 percent on the carrier news; AAA gasoline eased to $4.41 after a record September.

The Fire Pause: A War That Stopped Shooting

The defining fact of the week is an absence: after the sharpest escalation of the war, the shooting largely stopped. No US strike on Iranian territory ashore was reported for roughly twelve consecutive operational periods — the last was on September 1 — and Iran fired no ballistic missile at any host state for ten days and no drone for fourteen, the longest lull since the war resumed in July. The last host-state salvo remained the roughly twenty missiles that hit Al-Azraq in Jordan on September 8–9. Both sides, for now, are holding fire.

The one exception was maritime and contained: on September 14 US forces destroyed two IRGC boats in the Strait of Hormuz, which a US official said the Guard had used in an attempt to capture a Navy patrol drone. Around it, the fog thickened rather than the fighting. The IRGC claimed four MQ-9 shootdowns in three days, including over Qeshm Island, allegedly with a new air-defense system — but there is no wreckage, serial number, imagery or CENTCOM confirmation, and this dashboard keeps the claim tagged unverified. A reported Fox News account of a US-contracted vessel struck near the strait is likewise unconfirmed by the Pentagon. In a lull, an unverified claim is the most likely thing to break it on a false premise.

The Reckoning: UN War-Crimes Findings

With the guns quiet, the war’s most consequential event was a document. On September 17 the UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran issued report A/HRC/63/61, finding “reasonable grounds to believe” that US forces committed the war crime of launching an indiscriminate attack in two February 28 strikes: a Tomahawk that hit the Shajareh Tayyebeh primary school in Minab, killing at least 157 people including 120 children and 26 staff and wounding 95, and a precision-missile strike on a sports center in Lamerd that dispersed tungsten pellets and killed 22 civilians. The Mission also found that Iranian authorities committed crimes against humanity in suppressing the winter protests, citing 377 protest deaths including 56 children.

The reactions split cleanly. The White House rejected the report on September 18, calling the Human Rights Council’s work “unserious nonsense” and asserting that “the only party committing war crimes in the conflict has been the Iranian regime”; CENTCOM has separately denied responsibility for the Lamerd strike. The findings do not move this dashboard’s casualty counters, which record February’s dead rather than new September deaths — but they harden the sourcing on figures long carried as contested. Consistent with that discipline, the tallies stay source-attributed and unsummed: HRANA’s 3,636, the Foundation of Martyrs’ 3,468, JINSA’s 3,724-plus and a US/Israeli 6,000-plus estimate are surfaced side by side, never merged.

The Fed, Not the War, Moves Markets

For the first time in months, the biggest input to markets was monetary policy, not missiles. On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised the federal funds rate 25 basis points to a 3.75–4 percent target on a 12–0 vote — its first hike since July 2023 — with Chair Kevin Warsh warning that “inflation is too high and has been for too long,” explicitly tying it to Middle East energy costs. Sixteen of eighteen policymakers projected another hike this year, with a median year-end rate of 4.1 percent. The S&P 500 dipped to 7,551.81 on the decision, then rallied to close the week at 7,650.50.

The energy shock that drove the prior week faded. Brent, which had settled at $108.75 on September 15 as drones shut Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, eased to $103.87 by September 18 as Riyadh ran ship-to-ship transfers through Oman and moved roughly 2.8 million barrels a day through Hormuz over six days — about four times its August rate. The relief did not reach the pump: AAA’s national gasoline average climbed to $4.4761 by September 20, within about seven cents of the war’s May peak, with diesel still above $6.30. The independent tracker’s modeled war cost reached about $54.8 billion at $500 million a day.

Congress Constrains as the Lincoln Sails Home

The domestic front hardened. On September 15 the House voted 220–204 — its third war-powers resolution — to limit the President’s authority to strike Iran, with several Republicans crossing over, and Rep. Thomas Massie filed articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The votes are as much signal as constraint, but they mark the widest congressional pushback of the war — even as President Trump said the US was “nearing the end” of it and Speaker Mike Johnson pronounced the nuclear issue “taken care of.”

The force posture began to reflect the same de-escalation. After more than eight months on station, the USS Abraham Lincoln strike group left the Middle East, transiting from the South China Sea with its air wing and the destroyer USS Frank E. Petersen Jr. and pulling into Guam on September 19, bound for San Diego. That leaves the George Washington on station in the CENTCOM AOR and the George H.W. Bush in the Arabian Sea — a two-carrier presence drawing down toward one. Whether that drawdown is a bet that the fire pause holds, or a gap an adversary tests, is the question the next cycle answers.

  • Any new US strike on Iranian soil: None has been reported ashore for ~12 operational periods (last on September 1). A resumption, especially against nuclear sites, would mark the “different phase” VP Vance flagged.
  • CENTCOM on the MQ-9 claims: The IRGC’s claim of four MQ-9 shootdowns is unverified. A confirmed US loss over water would be the first Iranian surface-to-air kill of the resumed war and would undercut the standing ~90 percent air-defense-degradation assessment.
  • Brent’s range: Watch for Brent breaking back above its March peak near $119 (renewed supply-shock premium) or decisively below $100 (the war premium bleeding out). It settled $103.87 on September 18.
  • The Fed’s next move: With the first hike since 2023 delivered and a median year-end 4.1 percent penciled in, the October meeting and any further energy-driven inflation data will drive equities more than the front lines.
  • Whether the Gulf talks reconvene: The GCC–Iran ministerial collapsed after Bahrain’s withdrawal; watch for a rescheduled meeting and whether the unconfirmed Iran–Oman Hormuz-reopening plan produces anything real.
  • The war-powers resolution’s fate: Track whether the House’s 220–204 measure moves in the Senate and any veto, plus the Massie impeachment articles — the domestic constraint on the war is now a live variable.
