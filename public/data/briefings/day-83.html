The Negotiation Narrows to Uranium Custody

The defining shift of Day 83 is that the diplomatic file has narrowed to a single, much harder question. Three months into the war, the negotiation is no longer about whether Iran will stop enriching uranium — it is about who physically controls the roughly 440 kilograms of near-weapons-grade material Iran already possesses. Reuters reported that Iran’s Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, issued a directive that the highly enriched uranium must not be sent abroad under any circumstances. President Trump responded by publicly vowing to retrieve the stockpile. An Iranian official, in turn, told Al Jazeera that Tehran would “downblend” the material itself, inside Iran — diluting it to a lower enrichment level under Iranian custody rather than surrendering it. These three positions, taken together, define a deal space far narrower than at the war’s outset.

Pakistan’s mediation visibly intensified around this question. Field Marshal Asim Munir’s trip to Tehran — for “talks and consultations” with Iranian authorities, per ISNA — positions Islamabad as the operational channel carrying the latest U.S. response, which Iran’s Foreign Ministry confirmed it is reviewing against its own fourteen-point proposal. The structural problem is that the uranium-custody question is close to binary: a stockpile either leaves Iran or it does not, and an in-country “downblend” is precisely the outcome the Israeli intelligence community is reported to view as a deal-breaker. That assessment helps explain the sharper Israeli signaling of recent days, including a Channel 14 anchor’s on-air disclosure of a purported Iranian uranium-storage location — later walked back as “hypothetical” — and the IDF’s posture, restated by Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir, of remaining “on the highest level of alert and prepared for any development.”

The composite read is that the postponement diplomacy has bought time but not convergence. Trump said he is “willing to wait a few more days” to “get the right answers” from Tehran — a third consecutive deferral — and the thirteen-day zero-kinetic streak now stands as the longest of the war. But the narrowing of the file to uranium custody cuts both ways: it makes a deal more concrete to define, and also more brittle, because it removes the ambiguity that made earlier framework language easier to accept. If Tehran holds firm that the material stays in Iran and Washington holds firm that it must be removed, the “few more days” window closes onto a genuine impasse rather than a misunderstanding. The resumption decision still turns on whether Iran moves on the physical transfer — and nothing on Day 83 suggested it will.

Hormuz Becomes the Structural Battleground

If uranium custody is the headline dispute, the Strait of Hormuz is increasingly the one most likely to break a deal. On Day 83 Iran announced the creation of a “Persian Gulf Strait Authority,” warning that passage without permission would be subject to enforcement, and Iran’s Ambassador to France, Mohammad Amin-Nejad, told Bloomberg that Iran and Oman are “mobilizing all their resources both to provide security services and to manage navigation” through the strait — a description of a permanent toll framework. Taken together, these moves reveal a serious institutional play: to convert Iran’s wartime de-facto control of the chokepoint into a standing revenue authority that would outlast any ceasefire. This is no longer a tactical blockade tool; it is an attempt to rewrite the governance of one-fifth of the world’s seaborne oil.

Washington’s response was an unusually hard line. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said: “We’ve always said a tolling system in the strait would be unacceptable…It would make a diplomatic deal unfeasible.” That framing makes Hormuz tolls, arguably more than uranium custody, the single most likely deal-breaking issue — because while the uranium question could in principle be finessed through monitoring arrangements, a permanent Iranian tolling authority is a structural change to freedom of navigation that the United States has explicitly said it will not accept. The gap between the two sides on this point is not about verification or sequencing; it is about whether Iran emerges from the war with a new sovereign instrument over the strait.

The data underscore how much of the Hormuz picture is now narrative warfare. IMF PortWatch recorded just two vessels transiting the strait on Day 83 — roughly 2% of the pre-crisis baseline of about 95 a day — while the IRGC Navy claimed it had coordinated safe passage for 26 to 31 vessels over the same period. The collision between an independent count of two and an Iranian claim of more than two dozen shows Tehran manufacturing a narrative of normalcy precisely to legitimize the new Authority: if the strait is functioning under Iranian management, the toll regime can be presented as administration rather than coercion. For now the strait remains effectively closed, war-risk insurance premiums sit up to eight times pre-war levels, and the U.S. blockade continues in the opposite direction — CENTCOM said Marines boarded the Iranian-flagged tanker M/T Celestial Sea in the Gulf of Oman, the fifth vessel boarded under the blockade.

The Iraqi PMF Front and Lebanon’s “Always-On” War

With Iran itself in a nominal ceasefire, the war’s active escalation has migrated to the proxy front — and on Day 83 that produced an open diplomatic rift inside the Gulf coalition. The UAE called on Iraq to “immediately” prevent attacks from its territory after attributing the May 17 strike on the Barakah nuclear plant to drones launched from Iraqi soil; Emirati diplomatic adviser Anwar Gargash went further, publicly chastising regional “mediators” who he said were acting as “victims.” The numbers behind the friction are significant: U.S. officials cited by Al Jazeera put the cumulative count at more than 600 attempted attacks on U.S. and Gulf facilities launched from Iraqi territory since February 28. With Saudi Arabia and Kuwait having conducted retaliatory strikes inside Iraq, a sub-conflict is now being normalized on Iraqi soil that Baghdad appears too weak to suppress — the May 17 Barakah strike and intercepted drones into Saudi Arabia are the visible edge of it.

The Lebanon front, meanwhile, remains the war’s “always-on” sub-theater, inflating the cumulative humanitarian toll independently of the Iran-direct diplomatic state. Israeli airstrikes on May 20 hit Doueir, Tibnin, and Burj Shemali in the Tyre district, killing eight people — including three women and three children — while the IDF said it struck more than thirty Hezbollah targets and Hezbollah reported clashes around Haddatha, Biyyada, and Rashaf. The Lebanese Health Ministry’s cumulative toll since March 2 reached 3,073 killed, with 9,362 wounded and more than 1.6 million people displaced, roughly a fifth of Lebanon’s population; the ministry reported 31 or more killed in the preceding 24 hours. Israeli operations are expanding into the western Bekaa Valley, with entire villages reported destroyed.

The composite read is that the war now runs on at least three tracks that no longer move together: an Iran-direct front frozen in a compressed deferral cycle around uranium custody and Hormuz tolls; an Iraqi proxy front that is quietly escalating and straining the Gulf coalition from within; and a Lebanon front in open, worsening conflict. The naval blockade remains the one U.S. instrument operating without interruption — the CVN-72 USS Abraham Lincoln and CVN-77 USS George H.W. Bush hold the two-carrier steady-state in the Arabian Sea, down from three carriers before the Ford rotation, a quiet degradation of dwell that matters if other theaters test U.S. attention. Gulf mediation has bought time on the Iran-direct track; it has far less purchase on the Iraqi and Lebanese fronts, where the casualty count and the coalition friction climb regardless of what Munir carries between Washington and Tehran.
