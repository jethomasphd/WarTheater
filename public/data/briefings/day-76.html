Trump-Xi Day 1: A Joint Hormuz Position Emerges

The Day 1 readout of the Beijing summit produced the most consequential U.S.-China statement of the war. Per a White House official cited by CNBC, Trump and Xi “agreed that the Strait of Hormuz must remain open to restore energy flows through the critical waterway,” and Xi separately “reiterated Beijing’s opposition to the militarization of the energy artery and any effort to charge a toll for its use.” Both leaders agreed “that Iran can never have a nuclear weapon.” The Wall Street Journal independently confirmed the no-toll language. Layered on top of these substantive outcomes were the optics that diplomatic protocol partisans had been watching for: Xi descended the Great Hall stairs personally to shake hands with Trump, then individually greeted each member of a seventeen-person U.S. business delegation. Xi opened the substantive session with a Thucydides Trap reference, asking whether the United States and China could escape the historical pattern in which a rising power and ruling power produce war. The session framework was Beijing’s official English formulation: “a constructive China-U.S. relationship of strategic stability” — Xi declared that Beijing would treat that phrase as guidance “for the next three years and beyond.”

The Iran sub-track is now structurally Chinese. Within hours of the Day 1 readout, Iranian state television citing an IRGC naval official confirmed that Tehran has begun allowing approximately thirty Chinese vessels safe transit through Hormuz under IRGC management protocols, after formal requests from China’s foreign minister and Beijing’s ambassador to Iran. Bloomberg, Reuters, Fars, and Xinhua all carried the framing; the Wall Street Journal and Lloyd’s List confirmed that the May 13 Yuan Hua Hu transit paid no toll and used the IRGC-controlled northern corridor with transponder cycled off-on-off between Dubai anchorage and Larak Island. The result is precisely the no-toll outcome Xi articulated publicly, with Iran preserving operational control of who gets through.

Taiwan was the other half of Xi’s Day 1 substance, and the contrast with the cooperative Hormuz language was unmistakable. Xi called Taiwan “the most important issue in U.S.-China relations” and warned per Xinhua that mishandling it would put the relationship “in great jeopardy” — describing “Taiwan independence” and “peace in the Taiwan Strait” as “as irreconcilable as fire and water.” The structural read is that Beijing accepted being seen alongside Washington on a Hormuz/non-nuclear framework precisely because it secured an implicit U.S. concession on Taiwan rhetoric, even if not on policy.
