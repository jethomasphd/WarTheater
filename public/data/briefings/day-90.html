Situation Summary

Day 90 produced the most significant diplomatic moment of the war — and proved, in the same forty-eight hours, how little that moment has yet changed on the water. Negotiators reached a tentative sixty-day Memorandum of Understanding, first reported by Axios, that would extend the ceasefire, reopen the Strait of Hormuz to “unrestricted” shipping, commit Iran to clear all mines within thirty days, lift the U.S. naval blockade proportionally as commercial traffic resumes, bind Iran not to pursue a nuclear weapon, and open a U.S. track on sanctions relief and the unfreezing of Iranian assets. But the MOU is unsigned. President Trump has not approved it — at a Wednesday Cabinet meeting he said the United States was “not satisfied” and would otherwise “have to just finish the job” — and Iran did not confirm it; Iran’s Tasnim agency explicitly denied that any agreement was finalized. Lead U.S. envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner have a draft text; Vice President JD Vance said the parties were “making some progress” but were “not there yet,” and ABC noted any deal would need the approval of Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei.

The kinetic track ran directly against the diplomatic one. Late on May 27, at 10:17 p.m. Eastern, Iran fired a ballistic missile at Kuwait that Kuwaiti air defenses intercepted; CENTCOM called it an “egregious ceasefire violation” that “occurred hours after Iranian forces launched five one-way attack drones that posed a clear threat in and near the Strait of Hormuz.” The same day the United States struck a military site in southern Iran and intercepted multiple Iranian drones. Overnight into May 28, U.S. forces shot down four to five more Iranian attack drones near Hormuz and struck an IRGC drone ground-control station at Bandar Abbas; the IRGC Navy fired warning shots at four vessels attempting unauthorized transit of the Strait. Both sides, in other words, traded fire across the two days even as their negotiators drafted the text of a ceasefire extension — the “Strike-Talk Tango” at its most literal. The ceasefire holds only nominally; the MOU, as one read of the terms has it, is an agreement to get everybody to the table, with the hardest nuclear questions — highly-enriched-uranium disposal and enrichment — deferred into the sixty-day window.

Markets read the diplomacy, not the fire. The S&P 500 set back-to-back record closes — 7,520.36 on May 27 and 7,563.63 on May 28 — in an AI- and tech-led tape (Snowflake jumped thirty to thirty-six percent) largely decoupled from the war. Oil whipsawed: July WTI cratered to an $88.68 settle on May 27 (down 5.55 percent, a five-week low) as the market “bet on a swift end,” then rebounded about 2.5 percent on May 28 as the renewed strikes hit the tape, leaving Brent near $96.63 and WTI back above $91 but still tracking a second straight weekly decline. AAA national gasoline eased to $4.426. The human cost kept climbing on the Lebanon front, which is decoupling from the Iran track entirely: May 27 was one of the heaviest Israeli bombing days in weeks, with roughly thirty-one killed across Tyre, Saida, and Nabatieh and a forced-displacement order issued for all of south Lebanon; on May 28 an Israeli strike in Shuwayfat, south of Beirut, killed a woman, her baby daughter, and a Syrian child. No new official Pentagon war-cost figure was released, leaving the Pentagon’s roughly $29 billion (per Acting Comptroller Jules Hurst) against Harvard economist Linda Bilmes’s estimate of about two billion dollars a day and a projected one-trillion-dollar lifetime cost.

The 60-Day MOU: Diplomacy’s Biggest Moment, Still Unsigned

The draft sixty-day Memorandum of Understanding is the most significant diplomatic development since the war began — but the dashboard should not, and does not, record a deal as reached. Axios reported first that U.S. and Iranian negotiators had agreed in principle, and Al Jazeera and ABC, citing U.S. officials, corroborated a draft text. Per those accounts the MOU would guarantee “unrestricted” transit through the Strait of Hormuz — no tolls, no harassment — require Iran to clear all of its mines within thirty days, lift the U.S. naval blockade in proportion to restored commercial shipping, bind Iran not to pursue a nuclear weapon, and open a U.S. track on sanctions relief and the unfreezing of Iranian assets. The lead U.S. negotiators are Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. The first substantive issues inside the sixty-day window would be the disposal of Iran’s highly enriched uranium and the future of enrichment — precisely the questions that have blocked every prior framework.

Three caveats keep the moment provisional, and each is load-bearing. First, President Trump has not signed: at Wednesday’s Cabinet meeting he said the United States was “not satisfied” with the draft and would otherwise “have to just finish the job,” said he would not be rushed (“I don’t care about the midterms”), and explicitly ruled out trading sanctions relief for Iran’s enriched uranium (“No, no, not at all. Not sanctions relief, no”). Second, Iran has not confirmed: Iran’s Tasnim agency denied that any agreement was finalized, saying any Western narrative of finalization “is not valid” until announced to the Pakistani mediator, and ABC noted the deal would still require the approval of Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei. Third, the MOU is, by the accounts describing it, an agreement to get everybody to the table rather than a settlement — the hardest nuclear issues are deferred, not resolved. Vice President JD Vance summarized it: “We’ve made a lot of progress… they want a deal… we’re making some progress,” but “not there yet.”

The dashboard’s editorial posture, therefore, is to treat the MOU as a draft reached by negotiators, flagged TENTATIVE and UNSIGNED, not as a concluded ceasefire extension. One wrinkle is worth tracking: the duration. Current reporting specifies sixty days; an earlier Axios version in early May described a thirty-day window. If Trump signs, the dashboard should promote the story to a confirmed “ceasefire extended” lead and expect oil to gap down toward pre-war levels and gasoline to ease further. If the MOU collapses — on the Hormuz-tolling clause, the frozen-funds demand, or the uranium question — the escalation lead returns and Brent likely runs back above $100. The benchmark that would change everything is binary and known: a signature, or a major strike.

Strike-Talk Tango Goes Literal: Kuwait Missile and U.S. Counterstrikes Amid the Draft

What makes Day 89-90 distinctive is not that diplomacy and fighting coexisted — that has been the pattern for weeks — but that they ran simultaneously and at elevated tempo while the MOU text was being drafted. At 10:17 p.m. Eastern on May 27, Iran fired a ballistic missile at Kuwait. Kuwaiti air defenses intercepted it; no casualties were reported. CENTCOM’s response was unusually sharp, calling it “an egregious ceasefire violation by the Iranian regime” that “occurred hours after Iranian forces launched five one-way attack drones that posed a clear threat in and near the Strait of Hormuz.” Kuwait’s Foreign Ministry condemned the “sinful Iranian attacks”; its army said its defenses were “responding to hostile missile and drone threats.” The IRGC claimed it had struck a U.S. air base in Kuwait in retaliation for the U.S. strikes near Bandar Abbas — a framing that, like its earlier unverified MQ-9 and F-35 claims, U.S. officials reject.

The U.S. side struck on both days. On May 27 it hit a military site in southern Iran that officials said threatened U.S. forces and commercial Hormuz traffic, and intercepted multiple Iranian drones. Overnight into May 28 it shot down four to five more Iranian one-way attack drones near the Strait and struck an IRGC drone ground-control station at Bandar Abbas that, officials said, was preparing to launch a fifth or sixth drone. The IRGC Navy answered on the water, firing warning shots at four vessels attempting to transit Hormuz without its authorization, with Fars reporting missiles fired “from the south toward designated targets.” Each of these actions is individually small; collectively they describe a strait where the ceasefire is nominal and both sides exercise what they regard as “self-defense” or “sovereign control” on a near-daily basis.

The interpretive point is that the simultaneity is the signal. The same forty-eight hours that produced a draft ceasefire-extension also produced a ballistic-missile launch at a U.S. ally, U.S. strikes inside Iran, drone shootdowns, and naval warning shots — which is exactly why the MOU’s unsigned status matters. Trump has previously characterized one such exchange as a “love tap,” language that captures the calibrated, deniable quality of the fire; but calibration is not control, and any single miscalculation — a missile that is not intercepted, a drone that reaches a base, a vessel that is hit rather than warned — would test whether a draft on paper can survive contact with the water. The next seventy-two hours, spanning the Cabinet deliberations and the May 29 Lebanon-Israel talks in Washington, are the window in which either a signature or a rupture is most likely.

Markets Bet on a Swift End: Oil Whipsaws as Equities Set Records

The financial tape priced the diplomacy and looked through the fire. The S&P 500 set back-to-back record closes — 7,520.36 on May 27, by the narrowest of margins (+0.02 percent), then 7,563.63 on May 28 (+0.58 percent), a third consecutive record. The Nasdaq Composite climbed from 26,674.73 to 26,917.47; the Dow set a record 50,644.28 on May 27 and held near 50,669 on May 28. The May 28 advance was led by technology — Snowflake jumped thirty to thirty-six percent — in a rally that is, by now, structurally an AI-capex story rather than a war-relief trade. As CNBC framed it, the equity tape is treating a swift, recession-free resolution as the base case.

Oil told the more interesting story because it whipsawed on the news flow. On May 27, with deal optimism dominant, July WTI settled $88.68 — down $5.21, or 5.55 percent, a five-week low — as the market, in CNBC’s words, “bet on a swift end” to the war and a Hormuz reopening; Brent traded around $93.91. Then on May 28 the renewed U.S. strikes near Bandar Abbas and the drone shootdowns near Hormuz reversed the move: Brent climbed about 2.48 percent toward $96.63 and WTI recovered back above $91. Even after the rebound, crude was tracking a second straight weekly decline — the clearest market expression of the bet that the strait will reopen. UBS nonetheless raised its Brent forecast to $105, and U.S. crude inventories fell 2.8 million barrels on the week (API). AAA national gasoline eased to $4.426, about eighteen cents below the all-time June 2022 record.

The asymmetry in the setup is the analytically important part. Because the equity tape has already priced a deal, a signed MOU would likely deliver only limited additional upside in stocks while driving oil sharply lower — the move toward pre-war crude levels that the futures curve is already leaning into. The reverse is far more violent: a collapse of the MOU, or a kinetic escalation beyond the current “self-defense” tempo, would spike Brent back toward and likely above $100 and reintroduce the war-risk premium that May’s deal optimism has bled out. Defense names continued their war-driven run — Lockheed Martin rose from $531.14 to $537.21 across the two days and is up roughly fifteen percent year-to-date. The fragility of the oil bet, more than the records in equities, is the metric to watch: it is the cleanest real-time gauge of how much the market trusts a draft that neither government has signed.

The Lebanon Track Worsens: Forced Displacement of All South Lebanon

While the Iran track edged toward a draft, the Lebanon front had one of its worst forty-eight-hour stretches in weeks — and it is decoupling from the Iran negotiation entirely. May 27 was among the heaviest Israeli bombing days in weeks: roughly thirty-one people were killed across Tyre, Saida (Sidon), and Nabatieh as the IDF said it struck more than 150 Hezbollah sites. Most consequentially, Israel ordered the forced displacement of all of south Lebanon, extending earlier Nabatieh-area orders to Tyre, and Prime Minister Netanyahu said Israeli forces had advanced “beyond the yellow line.” The cumulative Lebanese toll is now reported divergently by source and timing: the Lebanese Health Ministry stood at 3,213 killed and 9,737 wounded as of May 26 (Wikipedia, The National), while CNN cited 3,269 killed and 9,840 wounded on May 27 — a discrepancy the dashboard carries with both figures and dates rather than synthesizing.

May 28 brought the war’s grimly recurrent signature — civilian deaths in a strike aimed at a militant. An Israeli strike in Shuwayfat, south of Beirut, killed a woman, her baby daughter, and a Syrian child and wounded fifteen; the reported target was Ali al-Husni, described as a missile chief in Hezbollah’s Imam Hossein Division. A separate strike hit an apartment building in Sidon, and the IDF claimed strikes on 135 Hezbollah targets in twenty-four hours. The fire ran both ways: an Israeli reservist, Sgt. Rotem Yanai, was killed by a Hezbollah drone in northern Israel. Lebanon’s displacement now exceeds 1.2 million people — about 22.6 percent of the country’s 5.3-million population per UN OCHA, up from roughly two percent before the war.

The structural significance is that the Lebanon war is being run on a separate U.S.-mediated track — direct Lebanon-Israel military talks were set for May 29 in Washington — that does not require Iranian consent and is not, on the U.S. reading, inside the scope of the Iran MOU. That raises the prospect of a “two-track resolution” in which the Hormuz, blockade, and nuclear clauses bind Tehran while Israel’s Hezbollah campaign continues under a parallel understanding. Tehran is on record — via the fourteen-clause framework its Foreign Ministry laid out earlier — that ending the war “on all fronts,” Lebanon included, must be part of any signed agreement. Whether that bifurcation holds, or whether a Lebanon clause becomes a precondition Tehran insists on, is among the clearest near-term tests of whether the MOU can actually be signed.
