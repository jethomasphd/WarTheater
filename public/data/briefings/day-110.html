The Fourteen-Point Memorandum: What the Released Text Says

On June 16, three days before the ceremony, Bloomberg, NBC and Time published the full fourteen-point text — the first detailed look at the deal Trump has called “all signed.” Its architecture is an interim framework, the “Islamabad Memorandum,” not a final peace: an immediate, permanent end to military operations on all fronts including Lebanon; mutual respect for sovereignty and non-interference in internal affairs; and a final agreement to be negotiated within a maximum of sixty days, extendable by mutual consent, and ratified by a binding U.N. Security Council resolution.

The mechanics are specific. On Hormuz, the United States lifts the naval blockade immediately on signing and restores traffic to pre-war volume within thirty days, while Iran removes “technical obstacles” and neutralizes its mines within thirty days; passage is toll-free for sixty days, with fees afterward pointedly not ruled out. Reconstruction is set at no less than $300 billion arranged as a private-investment vehicle, with Trump stressing there are no U.S. government grants. On sanctions, Washington agrees to terminate measures on a schedule, Treasury issues crude and petrochemical export waivers immediately, and roughly $24–25 billion in frozen assets is released during the talks. On the nuclear file, Iran reaffirms its NPT commitment and its estimated 440 kilograms of sixty-percent enriched uranium is addressed through on-site downblending under IAEA supervision, with the rest deferred to phase-two talks; Iran’s missile program and its support for regional armed groups are explicitly excluded from the agenda.

The text’s status is itself contested. Iran’s Tasnim called parts “inaccurate,” and White House communications director Steven Cheung said the leaked version “does not reflect the language of the actual MOU,” after which a senior U.S. official read out the fourteen points on the record. Iranian and U.S. accounts still diverge on tolls after the sixty-day window, on the size and timing of the frozen-asset release, and on the scope of phase-two negotiations; no prisoner-exchange provision appears in the released text. The Senate, meanwhile, rejected a war-powers resolution 47–48 — its ninth failure — underscoring how thin the domestic consensus remains beneath a deal the president frames as essentially his own.

Markets and Oil: A Three-Month Low as the War Premium Bleeds Out

With peace largely in the price, the mood on June 16 turned from euphoria to second-guessing. The S&P 500 slipped 0.57 percent to 7,511.35 and the Nasdaq fell about 1.1 percent as investors weighed warnings that the Hormuz reopening could take months and as the Federal Reserve’s June meeting got underway. The Dow, however, set yet another record, edging up 0.64 percent — a divergence that captured the moment: the war-risk trade has unwound, but the deal-execution trade has not yet begun. Defense primes and oil majors slipped as the premium bled out, while airlines and cruise lines gained on cheaper fuel.

Oil told the cleaner story. Brent settled at $78.96, its first sub-$80 close since March and a roughly five-percent drop, while WTI lost 5.8 percent to $76.05, a three-and-a-half-month low, after the Wall Street Journal reported Iran could begin selling crude immediately under the deal. Crude has now fallen nearly forty percent from the roughly $118 it touched at the end of the first quarter — which the EIA notes was the largest inflation-adjusted quarterly oil spike in data going back to 1988. The IEA, looking past the war, warned of a looming 2027 glut as supply growth outruns demand.

The relief is reaching consumers slowly. AAA’s national gasoline average eased to $4.025, still about a dollar above the pre-war baseline, and NPR and the AP cautioned that higher gas, grocery and flight prices will likely outlast the war itself. The bill is already large: Moody’s Analytics estimates the conflict has cost U.S. consumers and taxpayers about $132 billion so far, dwarfing both the Pentagon’s $29 billion official direct-military figure and the dashboard’s $34.44 billion phased tracker. For equity investors the asymmetry now runs the other way — a clean signing is mostly priced, while a deal collapse or a Hormuz mine incident could spike crude violently.

Hormuz and the Blockade: A Toll-Free Reopening, Pending the June 19 Signing

The centerpiece of the deal is the Strait of Hormuz. On June 14 Trump authorized what he called “the toll free opening of the Strait of Hormuz, and … the immediate removal of the United States Naval blockade,” and at the G7 he said the strait was “partially open” and would be “completely open” and “toll free” by Friday. The terms commit Iran to clearing its mines within thirty days and the United States to lifting the interdiction campaign that, by CENTCOM’s June 14 tally, had disabled nine vessels and redirected 142 since April 13.

The physical reopening, however, has not happened yet. Iran’s deputy foreign minister insists the closure remains in force until the deal is formally signed on June 19, and as of June 15 ship-tracking data showed traffic essentially unchanged — a wartime trickle far below the 130-to-140 vessels a day that crossed before the war. Kpler’s lead freight analyst projected that tanker entries to the Persian Gulf could climb to about twelve a day, roughly half of pre-war levels, within the first thirty days of the deal, with some 118 tankers currently stuck in the Gulf able to exit within fifteen days once the lane is genuinely open.

The naval picture that enforced the blockade is, for now, unchanged. USNI’s June 15 Fleet Tracker again placed both the USS Abraham Lincoln and the USS George H.W. Bush in the Arabian Sea, with the destroyer USS Spruance escorting Lincoln’s strike group; no U.S. carrier has entered the Persian Gulf. The benchmark to watch now is the mirror image of the one that mattered during the escalation: not a surge of additional combat power, but the drawdown of the blockade itself — the first concrete, verifiable test of whether the deal is being implemented rather than merely announced.

Contested Terms, and Lebanon the Live Spoiler

For all the talk of a completed deal, its terms remain publicly contested. Washington has described full nuclear dismantlement; Tehran’s own draft, circulated through Mehr, speaks of “no new nuclear commitments” and a sixty-day negotiating window rather than a finished settlement. The two sides differ even on Hormuz, with the United States describing a permanently toll-free strait and Iran reserving the right to charge “service fees” once the initial window closes. The roughly $24-to-$25 billion in frozen Iranian assets slated for release, and the sequencing of mine-clearance against blockade-removal, are the kind of details on which prior near-deals have stalled.

The most dangerous loose thread is Lebanon — the front that detonated the whole crisis. On June 14, even as the deal was being finalized, Israel struck Beirut’s Ghobeiri suburb, killing three and wounding sixteen; Trump said it was something that “should not have happened.” The Lebanese Health Ministry toll climbed to 3,783 killed and 11,699 wounded. Iran has insisted from the outset that any agreement end hostilities “on all fronts, including Lebanon,” and Israel — not a signatory to the memorandum, as Netanyahu pointedly noted — has shown no sign of halting its operations there.

That gap is the deal’s central vulnerability. The framework Trump has “signed” binds the United States and Iran, but the war’s ignition point lies on a front controlled by a party that says it is not bound. Diplomats from France’s Macron to U.N. Secretary-General Guterres welcomed the announcement as a step toward peace, and the market has taken it as real; but until the June 19 ceremony converts a digital memorandum into an enforced armistice — and until the Lebanon front actually quiets — the most accurate description of Day 108 is not “peace” but a fragile, well-advanced ceasefire awaiting its signature.
