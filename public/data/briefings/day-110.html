Situation Summary

Day 110 finds the war in a fragile diplomatic endgame. Two days before a formal signing scheduled for June 19 in Switzerland, the conflict has structurally transitioned from kinetic combat — the last U.S. strike on Iranian soil was June 11 — to a contest over implementation and terms. On June 16 the full fourteen-point memorandum of understanding text was released to the press, oil settled at a three-month low, and the Senate again failed to assert war-powers oversight. The dominant risk variable is no longer Iran versus the United States but Israel versus Lebanon, where Israeli strikes have continued straight through the memorandum’s rollout.

The released text is sweeping: an end to the war “on all fronts, including Lebanon”; a final deal within sixty days confirmed by a binding U.N. Security Council resolution; a toll-free reopening of the Strait of Hormuz with traffic restored to pre-war volume within thirty days; removal of the U.S. naval blockade on signing; a reconstruction fund of at least $300 billion framed as private investment; immediate sanctions waivers with roughly $24–25 billion in frozen assets released during the talks; and on-site downblending of Iran’s estimated 440 kilograms of sixty-percent enriched uranium under IAEA supervision. Its authenticity is already contested — Iran’s Tasnim called parts “inaccurate” and the White House said the leaked version did not reflect the actual MOU — and the venue is now confirmed as the Bürgenstock complex overlooking Lake Lucerne, not Geneva.

Markets have already priced the peace. The S&P 500 slipped 0.57 percent to 7,511.35 on June 16 even as the Dow set another record; Brent settled at $78.96, its first sub-$80 close since March, and WTI at $76.05 — oil now stands nearly forty percent below its conflict peak. Yet physical reality lags the politics: only about seven ships have transited Hormuz since the June 14 deal, with more than 550 still stranded. The human ledger’s lone moving column is Lebanon, where Israeli strikes killed at least fourteen on June 16 and four on June 17, prompting Trump to tell Benjamin Netanyahu to be “more responsible” even as he warned he would go “back to dropping bombs” if Iran misbehaves.

From Escalation to Armistice: How the War Wound Down in Five Days

The reversal was as abrupt as the escalation that preceded it. Through June 9–11 the war reached its most violent pitch since the opening weeks: after an Iranian drone downed a U.S. Army Apache over the Strait of Hormuz, CENTCOM ran three nights of self-defense strikes across Iran — Trump said the U.S. fired forty-nine Tomahawk missiles, some landing within forty miles of Tehran, and “dropped $250 million worth of bombs” — hitting air-defense, radar and command sites from Karaj and Isfahan to Bandar Abbas, Sirik and Gorgan. An explosion was reported at the Assaluyeh petrochemical complex, and two water reservoirs in Sirik County were struck, cutting drinking water to some twenty thousand residents in an incident Iran condemned as a war crime.

Then, on the evening of June 11, President Trump cancelled all further scheduled strikes and announced a “great settlement” he said had been approved by every regional party. The land war simply stopped. No U.S. munition has struck Iranian soil since; the only kinetic activity across June 12–15 was the interception of Iranian one-way attack drones near Hormuz, which CENTCOM said never closed the corridor. Iran’s Khatam al-Anbiya command had declared the strait fully closed to all shipping on June 11 and claimed to have struck two transiting vessels — a claim CENTCOM flatly denied — but the threat of a wider naval war receded as the diplomacy accelerated.

By June 14 Trump declared the deal “now complete,” and at the G7 the next day he called it “all signed,” with the memorandum of understanding executed digitally by the president and Vice President Vance ahead of a formal June 19 ceremony in Switzerland. Iran’s public posture was more guarded — spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei had said Tehran “has not reached a final decision,” and deputy foreign minister Kazem Gharibabadi maintained that the Hormuz closure stands until signatures are exchanged — but the trajectory across the five days was unmistakable: from open combat to an armistice awaiting its formal seal.

Markets Price In Peace: Records on Wall Street, Oil at Three-Month Lows

If June 10 was the day the market re-coupled to the war, the days that followed were the trade in reverse. As soon as Trump cancelled the strikes, equities staged a relief rally and never looked back: the S&P 500 rose 1.75 percent on June 11, added gains on the 12th, and closed June 15 at a record-territory 7,554.29, up 1.65 percent on the day; the Dow set an all-time high of 51,671.03 and the Nasdaq jumped 3.07 percent to 26,683.94. The VIX fell to 16.20, gold rose to $4,331, and the week’s tape read as a textbook de-risking on the prospect of peace.

Oil moved the other way, and hard. Brent settled June 15 down 4.76 percent at $83.17 and WTI down 4.87 percent at $80.75 — the lowest settlements since March 4 and a slide of roughly ten percent across the window — as traders priced out the Hormuz risk premium that had driven crude toward $126 at the war’s April peak. Pump prices followed with the usual lag, easing to a national average of $4.065, still about $1.07 above the pre-war baseline but firmly off the May highs. The dashboard’s modeled daily war burn fell back toward a $90-million standby rate as the strikes ceased.

The one group that did not celebrate was defense. Analysts noted the obvious irony that, for investors in the prime contractors that had rallied through the war, “the greatest threat is peace” — and the names duly softened as the deal firmed. The window’s other headline belonged to the IPO market: on June 12 SpaceX debuted on the Nasdaq, settling at $160.95, up more than nineteen percent from its $135 offer price, after raising $75 billion at a roughly $1.75-trillion valuation — the largest initial public offering in history, eclipsing Saudi Aramco’s 2019 record.

Hormuz and the Blockade: A Toll-Free Reopening, Pending the June 19 Signing

The centerpiece of the deal is the Strait of Hormuz. On June 14 Trump authorized what he called “the toll free opening of the Strait of Hormuz, and … the immediate removal of the United States Naval blockade,” and at the G7 he said the strait was “partially open” and would be “completely open” and “toll free” by Friday. The terms commit Iran to clearing its mines within thirty days and the United States to lifting the interdiction campaign that, by CENTCOM’s June 14 tally, had disabled nine vessels and redirected 142 since April 13.

The physical reopening, however, has not happened yet. Iran’s deputy foreign minister insists the closure remains in force until the deal is formally signed on June 19, and as of June 15 ship-tracking data showed traffic essentially unchanged — a wartime trickle far below the 130-to-140 vessels a day that crossed before the war. Kpler’s lead freight analyst projected that tanker entries to the Persian Gulf could climb to about twelve a day, roughly half of pre-war levels, within the first thirty days of the deal, with some 118 tankers currently stuck in the Gulf able to exit within fifteen days once the lane is genuinely open.

The naval picture that enforced the blockade is, for now, unchanged. USNI’s June 15 Fleet Tracker again placed both the USS Abraham Lincoln and the USS George H.W. Bush in the Arabian Sea, with the destroyer USS Spruance escorting Lincoln’s strike group; no U.S. carrier has entered the Persian Gulf. The benchmark to watch now is the mirror image of the one that mattered during the escalation: not a surge of additional combat power, but the drawdown of the blockade itself — the first concrete, verifiable test of whether the deal is being implemented rather than merely announced.

Contested Terms, and Lebanon the Live Spoiler

For all the talk of a completed deal, its terms remain publicly contested. Washington has described full nuclear dismantlement; Tehran’s own draft, circulated through Mehr, speaks of “no new nuclear commitments” and a sixty-day negotiating window rather than a finished settlement. The two sides differ even on Hormuz, with the United States describing a permanently toll-free strait and Iran reserving the right to charge “service fees” once the initial window closes. The roughly $24-to-$25 billion in frozen Iranian assets slated for release, and the sequencing of mine-clearance against blockade-removal, are the kind of details on which prior near-deals have stalled.

The most dangerous loose thread is Lebanon — the front that detonated the whole crisis. On June 14, even as the deal was being finalized, Israel struck Beirut’s Ghobeiri suburb, killing three and wounding sixteen; Trump said it was something that “should not have happened.” The Lebanese Health Ministry toll climbed to 3,783 killed and 11,699 wounded. Iran has insisted from the outset that any agreement end hostilities “on all fronts, including Lebanon,” and Israel — not a signatory to the memorandum, as Netanyahu pointedly noted — has shown no sign of halting its operations there.

That gap is the deal’s central vulnerability. The framework Trump has “signed” binds the United States and Iran, but the war’s ignition point lies on a front controlled by a party that says it is not bound. Diplomats from France’s Macron to U.N. Secretary-General Guterres welcomed the announcement as a step toward peace, and the market has taken it as real; but until the June 19 ceremony converts a digital memorandum into an enforced armistice — and until the Lebanon front actually quiets — the most accurate description of Day 108 is not “peace” but a fragile, well-advanced ceasefire awaiting its signature.
