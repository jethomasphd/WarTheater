The Fourteen-Point Memorandum: What the Released Text Says

On June 16, three days before the ceremony, Bloomberg, NBC and Time published the full fourteen-point text — the first detailed look at the deal Trump has called “all signed.” Its architecture is an interim framework, the “Islamabad Memorandum,” not a final peace: an immediate, permanent end to military operations on all fronts including Lebanon; mutual respect for sovereignty and non-interference in internal affairs; and a final agreement to be negotiated within a maximum of sixty days, extendable by mutual consent, and ratified by a binding U.N. Security Council resolution.

The mechanics are specific. On Hormuz, the United States lifts the naval blockade immediately on signing and restores traffic to pre-war volume within thirty days, while Iran removes “technical obstacles” and neutralizes its mines within thirty days; passage is toll-free for sixty days, with fees afterward pointedly not ruled out. Reconstruction is set at no less than $300 billion arranged as a private-investment vehicle, with Trump stressing there are no U.S. government grants. On sanctions, Washington agrees to terminate measures on a schedule, Treasury issues crude and petrochemical export waivers immediately, and roughly $24–25 billion in frozen assets is released during the talks. On the nuclear file, Iran reaffirms its NPT commitment and its estimated 440 kilograms of sixty-percent enriched uranium is addressed through on-site downblending under IAEA supervision, with the rest deferred to phase-two talks; Iran’s missile program and its support for regional armed groups are explicitly excluded from the agenda.

The text’s status is itself contested. Iran’s Tasnim called parts “inaccurate,” and White House communications director Steven Cheung said the leaked version “does not reflect the language of the actual MOU,” after which a senior U.S. official read out the fourteen points on the record. Iranian and U.S. accounts still diverge on tolls after the sixty-day window, on the size and timing of the frozen-asset release, and on the scope of phase-two negotiations; no prisoner-exchange provision appears in the released text. The Senate, meanwhile, rejected a war-powers resolution 47–48 — its ninth failure — underscoring how thin the domestic consensus remains beneath a deal the president frames as essentially his own.

Markets and Oil: A Three-Month Low as the War Premium Bleeds Out

With peace largely in the price, the mood on June 16 turned from euphoria to second-guessing. The S&P 500 slipped 0.57 percent to 7,511.35 and the Nasdaq fell about 1.1 percent as investors weighed warnings that the Hormuz reopening could take months and as the Federal Reserve’s June meeting got underway. The Dow, however, set yet another record, edging up 0.64 percent — a divergence that captured the moment: the war-risk trade has unwound, but the deal-execution trade has not yet begun. Defense primes and oil majors slipped as the premium bled out, while airlines and cruise lines gained on cheaper fuel.

Oil told the cleaner story. Brent settled at $78.96, its first sub-$80 close since March and a roughly five-percent drop, while WTI lost 5.8 percent to $76.05, a three-and-a-half-month low, after the Wall Street Journal reported Iran could begin selling crude immediately under the deal. Crude has now fallen nearly forty percent from the roughly $118 it touched at the end of the first quarter — which the EIA notes was the largest inflation-adjusted quarterly oil spike in data going back to 1988. The IEA, looking past the war, warned of a looming 2027 glut as supply growth outruns demand.

The relief is reaching consumers slowly. AAA’s national gasoline average eased to $4.025, still about a dollar above the pre-war baseline, and NPR and the AP cautioned that higher gas, grocery and flight prices will likely outlast the war itself. The bill is already large: Moody’s Analytics estimates the conflict has cost U.S. consumers and taxpayers about $132 billion so far, dwarfing both the Pentagon’s $29 billion official direct-military figure and the dashboard’s $34.44 billion phased tracker. For equity investors the asymmetry now runs the other way — a clean signing is mostly priced, while a deal collapse or a Hormuz mine incident could spike crude violently.

Hormuz: Political Reopening, Physical Standstill

The strait remains the deal’s centerpiece and its clearest reality check. The memorandum commits the United States to lift the blockade on signing and restore traffic to pre-war volume within thirty days, and Iran to neutralize its mines on the same timeline. But a U.S. military advisory on June 15 was blunt that nothing has changed yet: the blockade “remains in effect” until the June 19 signing, with mariners told “do not attempt to cross until explicit direction is given.”

The water bears that out. Only about seven ships have transited Hormuz since the June 14 deal, by MarineTraffic’s count, and more than 550 remain stranded; Windward logged roughly two dozen vessels around the strait on June 16, eleven of them running dark, while IMF PortWatch recorded zero AIS transits on June 14. Crude flows run about ninety-five percent below pre-war levels and LNG about ninety-nine percent below. The one tentative sign of motion came June 17, when TankerTrackers reported the first Iranian crude exports in two months crossing the old blockade line.

Even an orderly signing would not flip a switch. Mine clearance alone is estimated at forty to fifty days by shipping and security sources and at up to six months by the Pentagon; the minehunters USS Pioneer and USS Chief are staged in Singapore for exactly that mission, and France and Britain are to coordinate a reopening effort. UAE/ADNOC reportedly sees full flows only by 2027, and Seatrade notes that prior Hormuz “reopenings” have unravelled. The naval picture that enforced the blockade is, for now, unchanged: USNI’s June 15 tracker still places both the USS Abraham Lincoln and the USS George H.W. Bush in the Arabian Sea, with no U.S. carrier in the Persian Gulf.

Lebanon: The Live Spoiler Two Days Before Signing

If the deal has a fault line, it runs through Lebanon — the front that ignited the crisis and the one the memorandum can least control. Israeli strikes have continued straight through the agreement’s rollout: at least fourteen killed on June 16 and four more in southern Lebanon on June 17, following the June 14 strike on Beirut’s Ghobeiri suburb that killed three. Iran says Israel has now violated the Lebanon truce eighty-four times since the MOU and has warned of a “harsh response,” conditioning any lasting peace on an Israeli withdrawal.

The structural problem is that Israel is not a party to the agreement and rejects limits on its operations. Defense Minister Katz said flatly that Israel “will continue operating in Lebanon regardless,” and the framework Trump has “signed” binds only Washington and Tehran. That leaves the war’s ignition point governed by an actor who says it is not bound — exactly the gap that, as analysts put it, could “blow up the agreement.” Trump’s public response was to tell Netanyahu to be “more responsible,” a notably sharper tone toward Israel than the president has usually struck.

Trump’s own framing compounds the fragility. He has described enforcement in coercive rather than legal terms — “we’re going to bomb the hell out of them if they violate the agreement” — and warned he would go “back to dropping bombs” if Iran misbehaves, while conceding the MOU might not hold (“If it doesn’t work out, I’m blaming JD”). With the Senate’s ninth failed war-powers vote underscoring thin domestic buy-in, the most accurate description of Day 110 is not “peace” but a coercive, well-advanced ceasefire whose weakest seam is a front no signatory controls.

A slip would reverse the oil and equity moves and re-price escalation risk.
  • A Lebanon flare-up that “blows up” the deal: Israel killed four on June 17 and at least fourteen on June 16, is not a signatory, and says it will keep operating; Iran’s 84-violations claim and “harsh response” warning make this the most plausible spoiler before signing.
  • Whether the blockade lifts and Hormuz traffic recovers: Only ~7 ships have transited since the deal, 550+ remain stranded, and a U.S. advisory keeps the blockade in effect until June 19. Tanker counts climbing toward pre-war volume would confirm a real reopening; a continued trickle means the deal is not yet physically operative.
  • The mine-clearance timeline: Estimates run from 40–50 days (industry) to up to six months (Pentagon). Watch for MCM deployments — the USS Pioneer and USS Chief are staged in Singapore — and for the France/U.K.-led coordination mission to stand up.
  • The frozen-asset release and sanctions waivers: Roughly $24–25 billion in assets and immediate Treasury crude/petrochemical export waivers are “on-signing” items; their actual execution — and whether tolls return after 60 days — is the first economic test of implementation versus announcement.
  • Any resumption of U.S. or Iranian strikes: The last U.S. strike on Iran was June 11. Trump’s “back to dropping bombs” threat keeps tail-risk elevated through the 60-day window; any renewed exchange would flip the status from “ceasefire pending signature” back to active combat.
  • Whether a final deal materializes within 60 days: The memorandum is interim. A binding U.N. Security Council resolution, congressional review under the 2015 INARA, and the explicitly excluded missile and regional-proxy issues are all unresolved — and could stall the move from framework to treaty.
