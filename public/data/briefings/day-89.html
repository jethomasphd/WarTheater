The “Strike-Talk Tango” Stabilizes as the Operating Mode

The May 25-26 CENTCOM strikes are now the third meaningful kinetic exchange to occur during the April 8 ceasefire, and the pattern is converging on a recognizable mode rather than a drift toward collapse. The first exchange was the early-May Bandar Abbas and Qeshm air-defense engagements; the second was the mid-May tanker-disablement campaign against Iranian-flagged vessels in the Gulf of Oman; the third is the overnight Larak Island mine-laying interdiction plus the Bandar Abbas SAM site that had tracked U.S. aircraft. Both sides now have a vested interest in framing each event as “defensive” rather than “ceasefire collapse.” Iran’s Mehr News Agency said the Bandar Abbas situation “was under control,” not that the ceasefire had ended. CENTCOM’s Capt. Tim Hawkins built his entire statement around the word “restraint”: “U.S. Central Command continues to defend our forces while using restraint during the ongoing ceasefire.” Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei used the strongest Iranian language available short of declaring the ceasefire dead — “flagrant violation” — but Tehran did not move to declare it dead. Polymarket’s “Iran ceasefire continues” contract stayed in the high-nineties after the strikes were confirmed.

The Iranian counter-claim is itself diagnostic of the new mode. The IRGC, via IRNA and Fars, announced that Iranian air defenses had shot down one MQ-9 Reaper, fired upon and forced a second MQ-9 to retreat, and engaged an RQ-4 Global Hawk and an F-35 — all unverified, all explicitly contradicted by a U.S. official to NBC News saying “no U.S. aircraft were hit,” and all framed as the IRGC reserving “the legitimate and definite right” to respond. Twenty-four MQ-9 Reapers have already been lost over the course of the war per the U.S. Congressional report cited by EurAsian Times — about fifty-seven percent of all U.S. aircraft losses; a verified twenty-fifth loss would be a material datapoint, and CENTCOM’s silence on platform losses is itself notable. But the IRGC’s decision to push its retaliation into an unverifiable propaganda claim rather than into a kinetic counter-strike against U.S. blockade assets or Gulf shipping is consistent with the same Iranian incentive structure that Mehr exhibited: defend the Strait without breaking the ceasefire. The shootdown claim functions as an IRGC face-saving instrument, not as a kinetic threat.

The operational implication for the dashboard is that each strike should now be categorized as “managed escalation within ceasefire” rather than as a potential ceasefire-end event. The same two carriers, Lincoln and Bush, that conducted the deterrent screen remain the same two carriers that would conduct any resumption strike package; the language CENTCOM uses to justify a self-defense action against IRGC mine-laying boats is the same language it uses to justify every blockade-enforcement action since April 13; the rules of engagement now formally include mine-laying activity and live SAM coverage near the strait as triggers. The deliberate ambiguity is that “self-defense” in this context is operationally indistinguishable from preemptive degradation of Iranian Strait-of-Hormuz infrastructure ahead of a possible signing — CENTCOM gets the deterrent value of striking the mines and the SAM site whether or not the deal ever signs. The risk in the posture remains asymmetric: a “very small” strike that Iran chooses to absorb is a successful coercive signal; a strike that any IRGC commander chooses to retaliate against opens a kinetic re-spiral inside the deal window. The next 72 hours — particularly the Camp David Cabinet meeting Wednesday and the Lebanon/Israel direct military talks May 29 in Washington — will define whether the Strike-Talk Tango remains stable or whether the IRGC’s “decisive response” rhetoric translates into kinetic action against Gulf shipping or U.S. bases.

Iran Lifts the 87-Day Internet Blackout — The Strongest Non-Military Confidence-Building Signal of the War

The single most consequential non-kinetic event of Day 88 was Iran’s decision to begin lifting the internet blackout that had isolated the country from the outside world since February 28. Iran’s First Vice President Mohammad Reza Aref announced the decision on social media in the language of a deliberate policy step: “In line with the esteemed president’s mission and in fulfillment of the government’s promise, the first step toward free and regulated access to cyberspace has been taken.” The decision was taken at a May 25 meeting of the Supreme Council of Cyberspace under VP Aref. NetBlocks posted the confirmation on X: “Confirmed: Live metrics show a partial restoration to Internet connectivity in #Iran on day 88, after 2,093 hours of near-total isolation from international networks, the longest nationwide Internet shutdown in modern history.” The eighty-seven-day blackout is now the longest of its kind ever recorded — longer than the prior records held by Myanmar (2021) and Ethiopia’s Tigray region.

The timing and the actor are diagnostic. The decision was taken on the same day the United States struck IRGC mine-laying boats near Larak Island and an Iranian SAM site in Bandar Abbas. The decision was taken by First Vice President Aref operating within President Pezeshkian’s reformist coalition, under the convening authority of the Supreme Council of Cyberspace — the IRGC-aligned hardliners do not control the cyberspace authority and could not have blocked the decision unilaterally. The blackout was justified throughout the war as wartime information control, but Iran’s own Communications Minister Sattar Hashemi stated in late January (per IranWire) that “each day of restricted access has caused about 5 trillion tomans ($35.7 million) in direct economic losses” — meaning the blackout cost Iran roughly $3.1 billion in direct economic damage on top of the war’s kinetic costs. The Pezeshkian-Aref government did not lift the blackout because the war ended; they lifted it because they are positioning for the post-war reconstruction narrative.

The implication for the dashboard methodology is that a new category of indicator should be tracked separately: Iran-internal political signals. Domestic Iranian decisions to lift wartime measures — the internet blackout today, the airspace restoration the week prior, potential reopening of Iran Air international routes, eventual Hengaw / Iran Ministry of Health casualty updates — may lead diplomatic milestones rather than follow them. The Iran-internal track is the part of the war Tehran has the most control over, and what Tehran does inside its own borders is the strongest available signal of how the Pezeshkian government expects the framework to land. Lifting the blackout is also the first thing the Iranian Ministry of Health and Hengaw need in order to publish updated cumulative casualty figures, both of which have been frozen since April 19 and April 8 respectively. Watch for a major Iran MoH or Hengaw release in the next seventy-two hours: a fresh figure would mark Tehran’s acknowledgment that the kinetic phase is over, and the size of the increment vs the April baselines would itself be a political variable.

The Lebanon Track Decouples: U.S. Approves Israel’s Hezbollah Escalation Despite the Iran Framework

Day 88’s most underappreciated structural shift is that the Lebanon front is now being managed on a parallel diplomatic track that may not bind to the U.S.-Iran framework at all. Israel’s late May 25 / overnight airstrike on Mashghara village in eastern Lebanon’s Bekaa Valley killed eleven (per the Lebanese Ministry of Health) or twelve (per state National News Agency and the Associated Press, with twelve bodies pulled from the rubble), including a woman and two girls. The Mashghara strike was one of seventy-six IDF airstrikes on Lebanon on May 25 alone per Antiwar.com. Al Jazeera correspondent Zeina Khodr described the scene: “There were at least 10 attacks in the span of half an hour on an area with residential buildings.” The strike followed Prime Minister Netanyahu’s May 24 authorization of intensified Hezbollah operations after a Hezbollah drone killed IDF reservist Staff Sgt. Noam Hamburger; Israel’s decision-cycle is now operating on a 48-hour authorization-to-execution rhythm against Hezbollah targets in southern Lebanon and the Bekaa Valley.

What is new on Day 88 is the U.S. position. Channel 12 reported that U.S. Ambassador to Israel Mike Huckabee “approved” the expanded southern Lebanon operations — but cautioned Israel “not to bring down buildings in Beirut.” This is the clearest articulation to date of an emerging U.S. policy: a U.S.-Iran framework can sign even as Israel escalates against Hezbollah, provided Israel keeps its strikes geographically contained to the south and Bekaa Valley. Lebanon and Israel are also now scheduled to convene direct military talks on May 29 in Washington — a separate, U.S.-mediated bilateral track that does not require Iranian consent and does not depend on the MoU’s signing. Iran’s 14-clause framework, as Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei laid out on Day 85, explicitly includes “ending the war on all fronts (incl. Lebanon)” as one of the covered items — meaning Tehran is on record that the Lebanon track must terminate as part of any signed agreement. The U.S. position appears to be that Israel’s Hezbollah operations are not part of the Iran MoU’s scope.

The implication is that a U.S.-Iran deal may sign without a Lebanon component — a “two-track resolution” scenario in which the Hormuz, blockade, frozen-funds, and uranium clauses bind Tehran while Hezbollah operations continue under separate U.S.-Israeli understanding. The Lebanese casualty receipt is the structural problem with this approach: Lebanese MoH reported thirty-four killed in the past twenty-four hours per its X account on May 26, even as its public dashboard cumulative total stayed frozen at 3,185 since March 2 — a reporting lag that implies the true cumulative is closer to 3,219 by end of May 26. The war-to-date breakdown includes approximately 123 medics, at least 210 children, and nearly 300 women among the dead. If Iran signs the MoU while Israel continues operations at the seventy-six-strikes-per-day tempo Antiwar.com recorded for May 25, the resulting cease-fire-with-asterisks will be politically toxic for the Pezeshkian government in Tehran, the Salam government in Beirut, and the Aoun presidency. Bifurcating the Iran and Lebanon tracks may be the only way Trump gets the framework signed; whether that bifurcation holds once the Iran agreement is on paper is the next-cycle test.
