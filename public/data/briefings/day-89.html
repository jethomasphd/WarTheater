The Iranian MoU Leak: Negotiating Frame vs Negotiated Text

The single most consequential Day 89 development was Iranian state TV’s publication of a 14-clause Memorandum of Understanding draft committing Iran to restore Strait of Hormuz traffic to pre-war levels within roughly thirty days, alongside U.S. military withdrawal from Iran’s environs and lifting of the naval blockade. White House spokesperson Olivia Wales called the published draft “a complete fabrication” even as she affirmed “negotiations are proceeding nicely.” Both statements can be true simultaneously: the framework conversations in Doha that produced Iran’s January-style $24 billion frozen-funds-Phase-1 ask are real, and the specific clause text Iranian state TV published is not. The Iranian leak is a domestic political instrument — Tehran is trying to lock in maximalist baselines (full U.S. military withdrawal, blockade ends without conditions, frozen assets release as Phase-1) before the Iranian negotiating delegation actually trades those positions away for an Iranian demining commitment and uranium custody mechanism. The leak is also a signal to the IRGC and Mojtaba Khamenei hardliners that the Pezeshkian-Araghchi track is producing a deal Iran can defend in its domestic press as victorious.

Trump’s Cabinet response is the matching political instrument from the U.S. side. “Negotiating on fumes” signals to the GOP backbench — Senators Wicker, Graham, and Cruz publicly attacked the emerging terms as “JCPOA-resembling” — that Trump is in a position of leverage, not of capitulation. “Either that or we’ll have to just finish the job” restores the kinetic ceiling. “I don’t care about the midterms” signals that Trump is willing to accept short-term political pain to extract a deal he can label “great” rather than settling. The Abraham Accords contingency — making any Iran deal contingent on Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, Turkey, Egypt, and Jordan formally normalizing relations with Israel — is the maximalist regional architecture play that, if real, would push the framework past anything either side has yet acknowledged in the Doha track. The chain runs both directions: Iran leaks a text it can sell at home, the U.S. publicly hardens the asks it can sell to its right flank, and the negotiators in Doha keep talking about Phase-1 frozen-funds disbursement timing and Hormuz demining sequencing.

The structural fragility is that this two-track pattern — one negotiation in Doha, two domestic-political performances in Washington and Tehran — is what historically breaks down when the actual signing date approaches. Mojtaba Khamenei’s hajj-day Telegram statement that “the clock cannot be turned back, and the nations and lands of the region will no longer be a shield for American bases” is the most hard-line Iranian leadership posture since the April ceasefire and is at odds with the simultaneous Doha mediation track. The post-Khamenei IRGC-dominated leadership structure may not be unified behind the MoU track, which means a signed deal could still face internal Iranian rejection even after Trump-Khamenei sign-off. The dashboard read for the next seventy-two hours is that the gap between Iranian leak and U.S. denial is narrower than the gap between Mojtaba Khamenei’s rhetoric and Araghchi’s Doha brief — meaning that the binding constraint is not U.S.-Iran convergence but Iran’s own internal coalition. Watch for a public Mojtaba Khamenei statement endorsing the Doha framework as the single highest-information signal: until then, every “95 percent there” statement out of Washington and every “negotiating on fumes” quip from Trump may be talking past Iran’s actual decision-maker.

CSIS: Epic Fury Was a Strategic Raid on the U.S. Pacific Deterrent

The CSIS report “Rebuilding U.S. Missile Inventory: A Multiyear Project,” published Day 89 by Mark Cancian and Chris Park, is the most consequential domestic-policy artifact of the war to date. The headline finding: the 39-day Operation Epic Fury bombing and air-defense campaign against Iran depleted inventories of more than 1,100 JASSMs and more than 1,000 Tomahawks, and the Pentagon’s own current production rates do not support replenishment on a timeline consistent with a near-term Pacific contingency. CSIS’s framing is direct: “The United States has enough munitions for any plausible scenario in the Iran war, but the depleted inventories have created a window of vulnerability for a potential Western Pacific conflict.” The report also flagged that Japanese deliveries of 400 Tomahawks may now be delayed — an indicator that Pacific-theater allies are already feeling the second-order constraint. The Atlantic Council’s May 15 baseline, now superseded by the CSIS report, had reported approximately 45 percent of the Precision Strike Missile stockpile and 50 percent of the THAAD interceptor stockpile burned through.

The industrial response is in motion but lags the consumption. Per Raytheon’s February 4, 2026 mediaroom announcement, the company committed under framework agreements with the Department of War to ramp annual Tomahawk production above 1,000 units, AMRAAMs above 1,900 units, and SM-6 above 500 units, with “many of these munitions… growing 2 to 4 times their existing production rates.” Even at those expanded rates, replenishing the 1,000-plus Tomahawks expended in Epic Fury at a 1,000-per-year production cadence implies an at-or-near full year of replacement before stockpiles return to pre-war levels — assuming no additional consumption in the interim. The Patriot interceptor (approximately 1,000 expended), Precision Strike Missile (45 percent of stockpile), and THAAD interceptor (50 percent) lines all face longer rebuild horizons because of Lockheed Martin’s narrower production base and the long-lead components (radar electronics, propellants) involved. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao’s May 22 testimony to the Senate that the $14 billion Taiwan arms sale has been paused to preserve munitions for the Iran campaign is the operational confirmation of CSIS’s analytical finding.

The political implications are likely to dominate the next congressional cycle. The Pentagon’s pending $200 billion supplemental funding request to Congress (Washington Post March 18 baseline) is now structurally tied to the CSIS analytical framework: any vote on the supplemental will be litigated under the lens of “is this enough to refill Indo-Pacific deterrence?” rather than “is this paying for Iran?” Senator Wicker’s Armed Services chairmanship and Senator Cruz’s broader hawkish positioning give Republican backbenchers natural alignment with the CSIS frame even as they attack the diplomatic terms of the emerging Iran MoU. The Pacific deterrent cost is also a constraint on Trump’s “just finish the job” threat: a renewed kinetic phase against Iran would further deplete the inventories CSIS has identified as Western-Pacific-binding, which limits the kinetic credibility of the U.S. negotiating posture. Watch for an Indo-Pacific Command public statement on munition-readiness in the next ten days as the highest-information signal of how seriously the Pentagon takes the Cancian/Park finding.

Hormuz Reopens One Tanker at a Time; Lebanon Stays the Binding Spoiler

The Hormuz reopening track delivered its first physically observable evidence on Day 89. Bloomberg (Prejula Prem and Julian Lee, via Insurance Journal May 27) reported that “Commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz gained modest momentum over the past 24 hours as at least two non-Iranian supertankers exited the Persian Gulf… The Singapore-linked Eagle Veracruz and Greek-owned Nissos Keros were both seen having crossed Hormuz early on Tuesday… In total, five ships were seen crossing Hormuz in either direction… It’s the first time in a week that four million barrels of unsanctioned crude have been seen crossing the waterway.” The IRGC separately claimed via state TV that twenty-three vessels transited on May 27 alone, a figure CNN said it “could not independently verify.” The Bloomberg confirmation is the first meaningful non-Iranian commercial transit since the May 8 Sea Star III / Sevda disablements and the most concrete physical signal that the diplomatic track is producing operational change. Pre-war Kpler baseline was approximately 100 vessels per day; even the IRGC’s inflated 23-vessel claim represents roughly 23 percent of pre-war traffic, while the Bloomberg-confirmed two-supertanker transit is the first sign that non-Iranian-flagged vessels with non-Iranian cargo are returning to the waterway.

The Lebanon track simultaneously deteriorated. The Israeli Defense Forces struck more than 100 Hezbollah targets and conducted more than 120 strikes across Lebanon on May 26, with the Lebanese Ministry of Health reporting via National News Agency on May 27 that 31 were killed and 40 wounded in the prior twenty-four hours — pushing the war-to-date cumulative Lebanese death toll past 3,244 (Wikipedia 2026 Lebanon war May 26 baseline 3,213 plus the 31 NNA-reported May 27 delta). The IDF issued new mass evacuation orders for Nabatieh city on May 27. Prime Minister Netanyahu publicly vowed to “deepen operations.” The Lebanon/Israel direct military talks scheduled for May 29 in Washington now sit against a steadily widening Lebanese casualty receipt that includes approximately 123 medics, at least 210 children, and nearly 300 women war-to-date.

The structural read remains that Lebanon is the binding spoiler. Iran’s 14-clause MoU framework, as Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei laid out on Day 85 and reaffirmed in Tehran on Day 88, explicitly includes “ending the war on all fronts (incl. Lebanon)” as a covered clause. Hezbollah’s growing FPV-drone effectiveness against the IDF gives Iran a low-cost escalation lever it can exploit indirectly without directly violating its own ceasefire with the United States. The U.S. position — an Ambassador Huckabee-approved geographic containment of Israeli operations to southern Lebanon and the Bekaa Valley, with explicit warnings against striking Beirut — remains a workable bifurcation only as long as Hezbollah does not retaliate against U.S. assets in the GCC or against U.S. blockade-enforcement ships in the Strait. The next-cycle test is whether the May 29 Washington meeting can produce a binding ceasefire reset that holds for the duration of the Iran MoU’s signing window. If it cannot, the most likely vector for Iran-MoU collapse is a Hezbollah-on-U.S. incident in Lebanon or northern Israel that breaks the U.S. geographic-containment posture and forces Tehran to formally tie its MoU to a Lebanon clause Israel will not accept.
